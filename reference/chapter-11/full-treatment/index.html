Chapter 11 · The Middle

The Middle

Continue in Core

Narrowing, Delay and the Long Stretch: The Full Treatment

The Craft Behind the Story (Second Edition), Reference — Chapter 11. Part III: The Architecture of the Whole.

This chapter extends The Middle in Core. Section 1 restates the definitions so that this chapter can be read on its own. Illustrative passages are quoted briefly for the purposes of criticism and study. Section 9 gives the main sources. Where a work is read in translation, the translator is named.

Contents

  3. The extended catalogue of shapes, with three further shapes and a summary table

1. Scope and working definitions

The middle of a novel is the stretch between the first commitment the beginning cannot take back and the start of the ending. The ending comprises the climax and its aftermath, as The Ending (Chapter 16) explains. The first boundary is a change of state: the protagonist has entered the line of action the book will follow, and the opening’s normal is no longer available. The second is the start of the sequence that leads straight into the climax. By this definition, the middle is commonly half to three-fifths of a book. The proposed middle of the household novel runs from the register drawer at about 25 per cent of the book to Dov’s chapter at about 78 per cent, a stretch of 53 per cent.

The chapter’s mechanism rests on a small vocabulary. A complication is anything that stands in the protagonist’s way. An escalation is a complication that changes what the protagonist can do or what a move will cost her. The option list is the set of moves the protagonist can make towards the goal at a given point. An event narrows the list if it removes a move. If it makes a move cost more, it raises the price. An event that does neither only delays. The midpoint is a turn near the centre of the middle that changes what the protagonist wants or knows, how she acts (taking the initiative in place of reacting, for example), or how she fares. The value-shift log records what changes in each sequence of the middle. The writer uses two parallel sentences to record the change, as in The Purposes of a Scene (Chapter 1), and one word to name the stakes.

The governing claim is that a middle sags when its events leave the option list and its prices unchanged, however many events there are, and that its work is to narrow what the book can still do until the ending is the move that remains. A turn can narrow the book wherever it falls. Even a turn at the centre may leave every move open.

The chapter does not treat the escalation of a single scene-and-sequel pair (Chapter 2), the architecture of plot lines (Chapter 14), the protagonist’s inner change across the book (Chapter 12, Arc and Theme), the management of a revelation (Chapter 15, The Reveal), middles told out of order (Chapter 8, Non-linear and Multi-timeline Architecture), the middle volume of a series (Chapter 17, Series and Genre) or the order of revision (Chapter 18, The Structural Edit).

2. The theory at length

2.1 Aristotle and Freytag

Aristotle’s Poetics defines a whole as that which has a beginning, a middle and an end, and the middle as “that which follows something as some other thing follows it” (Butcher’s translation). The definition makes the middle a causal position: it depends on the beginning and produces the end. Two further passages bear on the middle. In chapter 9, Aristotle calls the episodic plot the worst kind. Its episodes “succeed one another without probable or necessary sequence”. Chapter 11 describes two elements that most move an audience. In a reversal (peripeteia), a situation turns into its opposite. In a recognition (anagnorisis), a character moves from ignorance to knowledge. Aristotle located both at the turning point of a tragedy, which in a short play is near its end. In the long novel they migrate. Elizabeth Bennet’s recognition in Pride and Prejudice, Till this moment I never knew myself, falls near the centre, in chapter 36 of 61 chapters. The modern manuals’ midpoint is in large part the old reversal and recognition moved to the middle of a longer form.

Gustav Freytag’s Die Technik des Dramas (1863), translated by Elias J. MacEwan in the 1890s, describes the five-act tragedy as a pyramid with the climax at its centre. Introduction and rise lead to the climax, and return or fall leads from it to the catastrophe. The pyramid fits the modern novel badly, since most novels place their climax late, and later diagrams that move the climax towards the end describe a different model under Freytag’s name. In Freytag’s original pyramid, the climax settles the direction of the action, and what follows is consequence. A novel’s midpoint does the same work at lower intensity.

2.2 Barthes’s delays

Roland Barthes’s S/Z (1970), translated by Richard Miller (1974), reads Balzac’s story “Sarrasine” through five codes. The hermeneutic code is the set of units by which a text raises an enigma and, after delays, answers it. Barthes distinguishes several kinds of delay. They are the middle’s tools, and what matters here is how each one affects the reader’s question. Where the household novel uses a delay, the table gives an example.

Delay What it does What it does to the question In the household novel
Snare Points the reader the wrong way Changes it, to a wrong one Elena’s inference that nobody had opened the till since March, which the smell of a biro contradicts (Ch 5)
Equivocation Mixes a true answer with a snare Changes it, ambiguously —
Partial answer Answers part of the question Changes it, by narrowing The looser hand in the ledger shows that someone kept writing. The question is now who wrote the entries, and why (Ch 6)
Suspended answer Begins an answer and breaks it off Holds it, with the answer in sight The father’s last entry, which stops halfway along its third line (Ch 6, Ch 13)
Jamming Declares the question unanswerable Ends it, without an answer The mother’s silence, which the book keeps unexplained by design (Ch 16)

Read as a set, the delays show why some waiting is pleasant and some is not. Partial answers and snares change the question, so the reader is moving even while the answer is withheld. A suspended answer holds the question still but puts the answer in view, which is tolerable once and maddening if repeated. A delay that repeats the same blockage, the question put and refused and put again, is not in Barthes’s list at all, because it does nothing to the question. It is the plateau’s delay.

2.3 Brooks’s detour

Peter Brooks’s Reading for the Plot (1984) takes Barthes’s account into a theory of narrative desire. Brooks calls the middle the dilatory space of the text, and in his chapter on Freud’s Beyond the Pleasure Principle he argues that the middle’s detours protect the end: an action that reached its end at once, by a short circuit, would arrive at an ending the text had yet to earn. The middle exists so that the end can be the right one. For a writer, the argument makes delay the medium of the middle. To serve the book, a detour has to bring the end closer. That is the narrowing claim in another vocabulary. What makes the detour bearable is the reader’s expectation that the end will give it meaning. Brooks calls this expectation the anticipation of retrospection, discussed in The Ending (Chapter 16).

2.4 The screenwriting conventions compared

Screenwriting manuals prescribe where turns fall in scripts of a given length. The table compares two such manuals with James Scott Bell’s advice for novelists. Percentages are of the script wherever a manual gives page counts.

Manual Extent of the middle Turns inside it What the midpoint does
Syd Field, Screenplay (1979) and The Screenwriter’s Workbook (1984) Act Two, pages 30 to 90 of 120, so 25 to 75 per cent A pinch at about page 45 (37.5 per cent), the midpoint at about page 60 (50 per cent), a pinch at about page 75 (62.5 per cent) Links the two halves of Act Two and refocuses the line of action; Field said he found it in Chinatown
Blake Snyder, Save the Cat! (2005) From the break into Act Two to “All Is Lost”, pages 25 to 75 of 110 (about 23 to 68 per cent) Fun and Games, the Midpoint at page 55 (50 per cent), Bad Guys Close In from page 55 to page 75 (50 to about 68 per cent) A false victory or a false defeat that raises the stakes
James Scott Bell, Write Your Novel From the Middle (2014) Not fixed in pages The mirror moment at the centre The protagonist takes stock of what she has become or realises that she is likely to die

All three manuals put a turn near the centre that changes the protagonist’s relation to the goal. Field’s pinch and Snyder’s closing-in show how the opposition’s actions surface as consequences the protagonist feels. Snyder’s false victory and false defeat are reversals of fortune; Bell’s mirror moment is a change of mode or of self-knowledge. The conventions leave a question for revision. Which moves does each turn close, and which does it make more costly?

2.5 Forms of publication

The manuals’ fixed midpoint is a recent convention. Earlier conventions grew largely out of how novels reached their readers. The three-volume novel of the nineteenth century gave its middle volume a natural turn: Pride and Prejudice (1813) has volumes of 23, 19 and 19 chapters, and Darcy’s first proposal falls in the eleventh chapter of the second volume. A novel issued in monthly parts, as most of Dickens’s were, needed a turn in each part. Such novels often read as a chain of small narrowings without a single midpoint. Great Expectations (1860–61), serialised weekly, marks its own structure instead, in three named stages (section 6.3). Television’s season structure has produced a further convention, the turn at about two-thirds of a season (section 6.4). The diagnostic tests ask what a middle does rather than where its turns sit, because placement conventions are period-bound and function is not.

2.6 The evidence

The empirical evidence is thin and indirect. Andrew Reagan and colleagues (EPJ Data Science, 2016) tracked the emotional vocabulary from beginning to end in 1,327 books drawn from Project Gutenberg’s fiction collection. The books, most of them fiction, yielded six basic emotional arcs: a steady rise, a steady fall, a fall then a rise, a rise then a fall, and two shapes with two changes of direction, rise–fall–rise and fall–rise–fall. The three arcs the authors single out for high median downloads all turn at least once: rise–fall, fall–rise–fall and two successive fall–rise arcs. The study counted downloads and traced changes in vocabulary. Whether readers prefer a turning point in the events of a story remains an open question. The chapter’s claims about narrowing are conjectures from editorial practice, offered as testable in a writer’s own draft.

3. The extended catalogue of shapes

Middles are classified here by the engine that carries their pressure and narrows the book. This section summarises the six shapes treated in the Core chapter, adds three more and compares all nine in a table.

3.1 The six shapes in brief

The escalating chain carries its middle on one line of action in which each sequence sets up the next and raises the price of going on. In the pure case, Highsmith’s The Talented Mr Ripley (1955), each of Tom Ripley’s acts is demanded by the last. The turned middle divides at a central turn into two halves that ask different questions; Catching Fire (section 6.1) and Pride and Prejudice are its cases. The stations middle links encounters by a constant such as a road, a list or a frame, and relies on a rule or limit to narrow the book. The picaresque, Don Quixote and The Midnight Library are its cases. A frame holds the stations middle of The Seven Husbands of Evelyn Hugo (section 6.2). The braided middle alternates plot lines and narrows by their crossings, as Chapter 14 describes. The accumulating middle gathers knowledge through Barthes’s delays; Collins’s The Moonstone (1868), whose successive witnesses each close some explanations of the theft, is its case. The contained middle holds its protagonist in a fixed situation and takes its engine from time and arrivals; Mann’s The Magic Mountain (1924), translated by John E. Woods (1995), and Towles’s A Gentleman in Moscow (2016) are its cases.

3.2 The mirrored middle

A mirrored middle is built symmetrically around its centre, so that events of the first half are answered, often reversed, by events of the second, and the centre is the axis. Fielding’s Tom Jones (1749) is the classic case: its eighteen books fall into three groups of six, one in the country, one on the road and one in London. At the centre, in Books 9 and 10, the novel’s lines of pursuit cross and change direction at the inn at Upton. Once the reader has felt the symmetry, she anticipates the answering half and reads each event against its partner in the first. Each event that answers its partner closes a little more of the pattern. The characteristic failure is symmetry that is visible to the writer and not to the reader, which produces a second half that repeats the first without the reader knowing why.

3.3 The interval middle

An interval middle is a gap. Between two blocks of scene, the book passes over a span of time or tells it briefly. The narrowing happens in the gap, when a move that had been open closes. Virginia Woolf’s To the Lighthouse (1927) puts ten years and several deaths into its short central section, “Time Passes”, and Shakespeare’s The Winter’s Tale puts sixteen years into a single speech by Time. The engine is consequence revealed at once: the reader returns to the action to find the option list already narrowed by events summarised or omitted. The characteristic failure is a gap that changes nothing. The second block resumes where the first left off, as if the time skipped had made no difference.

3.4 The retold middle

A retold middle turns at its centre by changing vantage: the second half retells the first half’s events from another position. Lauren Groff’s Fates and Furies (2015) gives the first half of a marriage’s story, “Fates”, largely to the husband, Lotto, and the second half, “Furies”, to the wife, Mathilde. What Mathilde reveals rules out readings of the marriage that Lotto’s account left open. Knowledge is the engine, as in the accumulating middle. Here the new knowledge comes in a single block when the teller changes, and that change is the turn. The characteristic failure is a second telling that adds texture without revising anything the reader believed, so that the reader rereads without being repaid. Multi-vantage structures in general belong to Chapter 13.

3.5 Summary table

The table gathers the nine shapes with their engines and their characteristic failures, as a quick reference for diagnosis.

Shape Engine: how it narrows Characteristic failure
Escalating chain Price, each step demanded by the last The escalation treadmill: price rises in size, never in kind
Turned middle A change of goal or knowledge at the centre The broken back: a second half that is another book
Stations middle A rule, a limit or a change in how stations are met The episodic middle: stations that could change places
Braided middle Crossings: one thread closes a move in another Two plateaus: threads that alternate without touching
Accumulating middle Partial answers and snares that change the question The plateau of interviews: answers that leave the question where it was
Contained middle Time and arrivals The holding pattern: waiting for a scheduled event
Mirrored middle Anticipation of the answering half Symmetry the reader cannot see
Interval middle Consequence revealed after a gap A gap that changes nothing
Retold middle Revision of what the reader believed A second telling that revises nothing

A book can combine several shapes. In revision, ask which engine carries the pressure in each half of the middle. The household novel’s middle is braided and accumulating, and its revision (section 7) makes it braided and turned.

4. The extended catalogue of failure patterns

The Core chapter gives five patterns in full, each with a published exception; they are summarised here and followed by six more. For each additional pattern the entry gives symptom, mechanism and remedy.

4.1 The five patterns of the Core chapter

The plateau repeats one value shift at constant stakes, leaving the option list unchanged; its exception is Beckett’s Waiting for Godot, which Vivian Mercier called a play in which “nothing happens, twice”, and in which Pozzo and Lucky’s return makes the cost of the repeated wait visible. The episodic middle joins its sequences by a constant rather than cause; its exception is Don Quixote, translated by Edith Grossman (2003), whose second part supplies a cause the first lacked. The absent opposition lets the opposing force vanish while the protagonist prepares; its exception is Moby-Dick (1851), whose pursuit carries the middle through reports and encounters with other ships. The Pequod’s chase begins in chapter 133 of 135 chapters. The holding pattern waits for an event scheduled at its end; its exception is Tartt’s The Goldfinch (2013), whose Las Vegas stretch conceals the book’s central plant. The broken back turns so hard at the centre that the second half is another book; its exception is Hitchcock’s Psycho (1960), which kills its protagonist about 43 per cent of the way through and sinks the first half’s question in a swamp on screen.

4.2 The training-montage middle

Symptom. The protagonist spends the middle acquiring the skill, knowledge or equipment the climax will need: lessons, rehearsals, research, a journey to the place of confrontation.

Mechanism. Acquisition opens moves rather than closing them, so the option list grows through the middle and the ending must close everything at once. If the climax requires the new skill, knowledge or equipment, the reader expects the protagonist to acquire it. The question that could supply pressure is what she stands to lose by doing so.

Remedy. Make each acquisition cost a move: the skill learned at the price of an ally, the knowledge at the price of a secret kept. Or summarise the acquisition and spend the middle on what it costs.

4.3 The spectacle that turns nothing

Symptom. The middle’s largest set piece, a battle, a party, a storm, a death, sits at or near the centre, and the book after it continues as before.

Mechanism. The event is large, but the reader sees that it leaves the option list unchanged. Once she has seen a large event pass without consequence, she learns to discount size. The set piece occupies the place of a turn without doing its work.

Remedy. Run the midpoint test on the set piece. If the set piece leaves the protagonist’s goal, knowledge, mode and fortune unchanged, give it a consequence that closes a move or place it away from the centre, where the reader is less likely to mistake it for the turn.

4.4 The reset

Symptom. Each sequence ends by restoring the situation it began with: the danger averted, the quarrel made up, the secret kept for another day.

Mechanism. The reset is the norm of episodic series television, in which each episode returns its characters to a stable state so that episodes can be watched in any order. In a novel it produces a middle of self-cancelling sequences, each of which leaves the option list as the previous sequence left it. The result is a plateau achieved by repair rather than repetition.

Remedy. Let something survive each sequence: a cost paid, a fact known, a relation changed. The reset is acceptable as an occasional rest, and a book can use one to show that a character cannot change.

4.5 The research middle

Symptom. The middle devotes whole sequences to what the writer learned about the history of the setting, the workings of the trade or the procedures of the institution.

Mechanism. The information arrives without pressure, in the terms of the pressure test in The Purposes of a Scene (Chapter 1). Where Barthes’s partial answer narrows a question the reader is already asking, the research middle answers questions she has yet to ask.

Remedy. Attach each piece of information to a move it opens or closes for the protagonist, and cut what cannot be attached. Some readers take pleasure in the information itself. For those who love the cetology chapters of Moby-Dick, the facts give the voice its texture.

4.6 The second sag

Symptom. A strong midpoint sets the question for the second half, but no further moves close until the climax.

Mechanism. The midpoint has done its narrowing at once, and the writer, relieved, lets the clock or the coming climax carry the rest. A first revision that installs a midpoint can leave this sag if the writer stops checking at the turn.

Remedy. Compare the option list at the midpoint with the list three-quarters of the way through the book. Give the stretch between them at least one sequence that closes a move, ideally as a consequence of the opposition’s actions. The household revision still has a second sag (section 7).

4.7 The premature climax

Symptom. The middle discharges the book’s central conflict, and the second half restarts it, often with a new antagonist or a higher stake.

Mechanism. Brooks’s short circuit: the action has reached an end too early, and the second half must manufacture a new middle to reach the real one. Readers feel the book has ended and begun again.

Remedy. Decide which climax is the book’s. If the first is the climax, shorten what follows to the aftermath the book needs, as The Ending (Chapter 16) describes in its account of proportion. If the second is the climax, recast the first as a turn that leaves the conflict open.

5. The diagnostic procedures

For the Core chapter’s eight tests, prepare a summary of the middle. Give each sequence one line, keep the sequences in order and note each one’s position as a percentage of the book. Four tests repay a fuller description.

The value-shift log (test 1). For each sequence, write two parallel sentences, one for what is true at the start and one for what is true at the close. Name the stakes in one word. Give the log four columns for position, sequence, shift and stakes. In the shift column, mark each pair of neighbouring sequences that repeat the same kind of change. In the stakes column, mark each pair that repeats the same stake. A pair marked in both columns is a plateau, and a run of three sequences is a plateau the reader is likely to notice. The log brings the redundancy test of The Purposes of a Scene (Chapter 1) to the scale of the book. The scene test asks whether the reader has already received this shift.

The option list (test 2). List the moves open to the protagonist at the end of the beginning, at the midpoint and at the start of the ending. Add a fourth list three-quarters of the way through the book to check for a second sag. Include any move the protagonist has declined that she could still make. When a move disappears between lists, note where it closed and whether the opposition, circumstance or the protagonist’s own choice was responsible. If the list holds steady, revisit that stretch. If only the protagonist closes moves, check whether the opposition has exerted any pressure on her choices. If several moves remain open at the start of the ending, the ending will read as one choice among several. The household lists are in section 7.

The shuffle (test 3). Write each sequence on a card and shuffle the cards. Try to put them back in order a week later, or give them to a reader who has not seen the summary and ask her to do it. Work only from what is on the cards. Where the order cannot be recovered, look for a missing causal link or, in a stations middle, for a rule that would fix the order.

The opposition’s calendar (test 5). Date the opposing force’s actions through the middle as if you were drawing up the timeline of its own book. Mark each action that appears in a scene or makes the protagonist feel a consequence. Any quarter of the middle without a mark fails the opposition test. An action that leaves the protagonist’s options and their prices unchanged is a reminder rather than a pinch.

6. Further analyses

6.1 Catching Fire: the turn that closes every move

Suzanne Collins’s Catching Fire (2009), the second novel of the Hunger Games trilogy, is in three parts of nine chapters each: “The Spark”, “The Quell” and “The Enemy”. The first part holds Katniss Everdeen to the same task at home and on a tour of the other districts. She tries to placate the president she defied at the end of the first novel, chiefly by staging an engagement with Peeta Mellark for the cameras, while unrest spreads. In chapter 12 the president announces the rules of the Quarter Quell, the anniversary Games: the tributes will be reaped from each district’s existing pool of victors. As her district’s only female victor, Katniss is certain to return to the arena.

The announcement closes off placation, flight and private life at a stroke. Katniss’s goal shifts from surviving the regime’s suspicion to surviving the arena again. The announcement falls in chapter 12 of 27 chapters (12 ÷ 27, about 44 per cent by chapter count). The reader has spent two chapters in the second part before learning what its title means for Katniss. The turn answers the first half’s question of whether placating the regime can keep Katniss safe. The answer is no, and that answer sends her back into the arena, so the turn holds the two halves together.

6.2 The Seven Husbands of Evelyn Hugo: stations held by a frame

Taylor Jenkins Reid’s The Seven Husbands of Evelyn Hugo (2017) is a stations middle in its plainest form. An ageing film star, Evelyn Hugo, gives a little-known journalist, Monique Grant, the exclusive right to write her life, and tells it in seven parts, each named for a husband with an epithet in the title, such as “gullible Mick Riva”. The husbands could be read as an episodic list, and their order is fixed only by chronology. Two engines hold the middle. The frame asks why Evelyn has chosen Monique. Only at the end does Monique learn that her father died in the crash that also killed one of Evelyn’s husbands, and that Evelyn arranged the scene to protect her husband’s memory. The other engine is the constant hidden behind the list, Evelyn’s long relationship with the actress Celia St. James. Each marriage serves, conceals or damages that relationship, making the husbands stations on a road whose destination lies elsewhere. The book shows what a stations middle needs when its stations cannot narrow: a question outside the list that every station brings closer.

6.3 The second stage of Great Expectations

Dickens’s Great Expectations (1860–61) divides its 59 chapters into three named stages of Pip’s expectations, and the division marks a turned middle with unusual explicitness. The first stage (chapters 1 to 19) ends as Pip leaves the forge for London with his expectations. In the second stage (chapters 20 to 39), the social price rises as Pip spends, borrows and grows ashamed of Joe. That stage ends when the convict Magwitch returns and reveals that he is Pip’s benefactor. The turn falls in chapter 39 of 59 chapters (39 ÷ 59, about 66 per cent by chapter count). It is a recognition in Aristotle’s sense. Pip has believed that his fortune came from Miss Havisham, and learning otherwise overturns his sense of what it was for. The third stage plays out the consequences. A novel can turn this late when those consequences need more room than the approach to the turn.

6.4 Squid Game: narrowing by turning an alliance into an opposition

The first season of Squid Game (Netflix, 2021), created by Hwang Dong-hyuk, runs for nine episodes. Players in debt compete in children’s games, and the losers die. As the season goes on, alliances give the players their best chance of survival. The sixth episode, “Gganbu”, turns that move against them. Told to form pairs, the players choose partners they trust. Only then do they learn that they will play against each other and that the rules require one player in each pair to die. Episode 6 of 9 puts this turn two-thirds of the way through the season by episode count. The middle has taught the viewer to count on alliances, and now each player faces the partner she chose. The episode’s most telling scenes show the partners deciding what to do with that knowledge. Ji-yeong lets her partner win. Gi-hun tries to cheat Il-nam, who sees through the trick and gives him the last marble anyway. Any middle that has taught the reader to rely on a move can narrow by turning that move into its opposite.

7. The household middle in full

The Core chapter’s revision case gives the draft middle, the tests, the revised sequence and two passages. This section adds the option lists for each sibling and explains what the revision would change in the household novel.

The percentages in this proposed revision mark approximate positions in the book. At about 25 per cent the drawer chapter ends with the ledger and the looser hand (Chapter 6). At about 30 per cent Dov’s thread, seen from outside, shows the buyer’s demand that the building, shop and flat be emptied by the end of the month (Chapter 14). At about 38 per cent Elena comes back to finish reading and finds the drawer empty. A boy knocks on the glass about the sign, which still reads OPEN. She waves him off and turns the sign to CLOSED. On the way home she decides not to ask Dov who took the ledger. At about 50 per cent, the midpoint, both sisters receive Dov’s request to clear the flat by the end of the month. He gives no reason. At about 66 per cent Nadia, clearing the flat, puts her hand into the pocket of the coat on its hook and finds the good pen (Chapter 7). At about 78 per cent Dov’s chapter, set on the evening of 9 March (Chapter 13), begins the climax.

The option lists track what each sibling can still do. Elena wants to know why the locks were changed and what the ledger holds, Nadia wants to keep the coat and the flat as they are, and Dov wants to sell before another season of losses. Elena closes one move by deciding against asking Dov. Nadia loses the option of leaving the flat alone when Dov makes his request, and Dov closes his own way back when he signs. The table also shows the second sag the Core case records. From 66 to 78 per cent of the book, the draft shows no further change in Elena’s options, and for Nadia the only change is that the deadline draws nearer. For twelve per cent of the book, the clock alone holds the middle.

The changes this revision would make to the household canon are recorded in C21 for the author to confirm. The revision gives the empty drawer, the request and the signing approximate positions, and places the request before the signing, an order The Subplot (Chapter 14) left open. It gives Elena a decision not to ask Dov, which keeps her from putting her question to him until the convergence. The scene at the drawer pays two details from the prologue by rhyme. Elena waves a boy away from the glass as her father did, then turns the sign from OPEN to CLOSED, the move her father never made. The novel leaves open whether the boys are the same. The revision also makes Dov’s unanswered calls to Nadia in the opening the carrier of the midpoint. The new element is the voicemail she plays.

8. The limits of the model

The narrowing model describes middles that move towards a single end, and it describes them well. Three kinds of book fit it less well. Novels of duration, The Magic Mountain or The Rings of Saturn, prefer the experience of time to the closing of possibilities, and the model will report them as failures of the clock or of the midpoint. Novels of accumulation without convergence, the large social novel whose threads never meet, narrow nothing in particular and gain their force from extent. And comic novels of digression, Tristram Shandy or Three Men in a Boat, delay for the pleasure of the delay. For these books the tests still show where the options stay open. A writer can use that result to describe the middle she has built and judge it by the pleasure she wants it to give.

A second limit concerns the reader. In this model, momentum is the rate at which the range of endings the reader can imagine shrinks. That is a conjecture from editorial experience, consistent with Brooks. Readers differ in how many endings they hold in mind, and a genre can supply an expectation in advance. A reader of a classic detective novel expects an answer from the first page and feels the book narrow as the list of suspects grows shorter.
