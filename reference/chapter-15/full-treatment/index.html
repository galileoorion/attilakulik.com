Chapter 15 · The Reveal

The Reveal

Continue in Core

Disclosure, Withholding and the Second Reading: The Full Treatment

The Craft Behind the Story (Second Edition), Reference — Chapter 15. Part III: The Architecture of the Whole.

This chapter extends the Core chapter The Reveal. Section 1 restates the definitions so that this chapter can be read on its own. Quotations are brief and serve criticism and study. Section 10 lists the sources and identifies the translations used. The analyses disclose the endings of the works they discuss.

1. Scope and working definitions

A reveal discloses a fact that changes the reader’s understanding of what she has already read. New information can change what the reader expects to happen next. What makes a disclosure a reveal is the change it makes to what she has already read. The first reading is what she understood when she came to a passage, and the second reading is what she understands once she knows the truth. She may look back in memory or reread the passage. The movement between them is the double reading. A reveal is prepared when the passages before it support both readings.

A recognition, Aristotle’s anagnorisis, is a character’s change from ignorance to knowledge. When a reveal and a recognition coincide, the reader and the character learn the truth together. Sometimes the reader learns alone. Sometimes a character discovers what the reader already knew.

A reveal is earned when the passages before it bear two readings and the book gives a reason for the delay. The reader may follow a character who does not know the truth, for instance, or a teller who has a reason to keep it back. A reveal may reinterpret as far back as those two readings hold. It may falsify only passages a teller in the book could have shaped. These conditions concern preparation and authority. A reveal the reader half expects can satisfy them, and so can a reveal in the first quarter of the book.

This chapter asks when and how the reader learns the truth. Chapter 7 explains how to narrate a past event once a reveal makes that necessary, and Chapter 8, Non-linear and Multi-timeline Architecture explains how the arrangement of timelines can withhold information. Chapter 10 treats the prologue that discloses the ending in advance, and Chapter 11 the placing of a midpoint. Chapter 12 examines the character’s change of rule, and Chapter 13 asks which minds the reader shares and what those minds can know. Chapter 16 treats the ending’s other duties. Chapter 17, Series and Genre discusses reveals held across volumes and genres that require a reveal.

2. The theory at length

2.1 Aristotle’s recognitions

The Poetics (S. H. Butcher’s translation) defines recognition in chapter 11 as “a change from ignorance to knowledge, producing love or hate between the persons destined by the poet for good or bad fortune”, and calls the finest recognition one attended by a reversal, as in the Oedipus. Chapter 16 ranks the kinds. Lowest are recognitions by signs such as birthmarks, scars and necklaces. Aristotle calls this “the least artistic form” and says it is most often used “from poverty of wit”. Next are recognitions invented at will by the poet, as when Orestes simply declares himself. Third is recognition by memory, when a sight or a song awakens a feeling. Fourth is recognition by reasoning, as in the Choephori: “Some one resembling me has come: no one resembles me but Orestes: therefore Orestes has come.” A fifth, which Aristotle calls composite, turns on a false inference by one of the characters. Best of all is the recognition that “arises from the incidents themselves, where the startling discovery is made by natural means”.

The ranking anticipates two of this chapter’s tests. A token the story has done nothing to prepare, or a recognition the poet simply declares, gives a reveal from outside (test 8). When recognition arises from the incidents, the book’s own chain of events produces the discovery. The pairing of recognition with reversal anticipates test 6 (Consequence). In Aristotle’s finest instance, the discovery changes the discoverer’s fortune in the same movement.

2.2 Sternberg’s three interests and Barthes’s hermeneutic code

In Expositional Modes and Temporal Ordering in Fiction (1978) and his later Poetics Today essays “Telling in Time”, Meir Sternberg derives narrative interest from the relation between the order of events and the order of telling. Suspense is uncertainty about the future of the story. Curiosity is uncertainty about a past the reader knows to be missing. Surprise is the discovery of a gap the reader did not know existed. A single fact can generate any of the three interests, depending on what the telling lets the reader know and when. A curiosity reveal answers a question the book has kept alive and invited the reader to guess at. A surprise reveal depends on passages that bear two readings without advertising the second. A reader who guesses that second reading loses the surprise.

In his reading of Balzac’s novella Sarrasine, Roland Barthes calls the means by which a text poses enigmas and delays their answers the hermeneutic code (S/Z, 1970, translated by Richard Miller in 1974). The devices he lists include the thematisation and proposal of the enigma, its formulation, the promise of an answer, the snare (a deliberate evasion of the truth), the equivocation (a mixture of truth and snare), jamming (an acknowledgement that the enigma is insoluble), the suspended answer, the partial answer, and the disclosure. The list is a repertoire for the space between a question and its answer. In the terms used here, the snare is akin to the decoy reading, and Barthes’s equivocation to a sentence that is true under both readings. Barthes’s jamming is the declined reveal seen from inside a sentence.

2.3 Hitchcock’s bomb

In his interviews with François Truffaut (Hitchcock, 1966, English edition 1967), Alfred Hitchcock distinguished surprise from suspense with a bomb under a table. An explosion without warning gives the audience fifteen seconds of surprise. Show the bomb in advance and let the audience know it will go off at one o’clock, and an ordinary conversation can give it fifteen minutes of suspense. The difference is one of alignment, or whether the audience learns a fact before, with or after a character. In Vertigo (1958), Judy’s flashback lets the audience in on the deception before Scottie learns of it. Hitchcock told Truffaut that nearly everyone around him had opposed the early disclosure. In Psycho (1960), he lets Marion’s death come as a surprise. The writer chooses which interest the audience will have in the scene.

2.4 The fair-play codes

The golden-age detective story made explicit what a reveal owes the reader. S. S. Van Dine’s “Twenty Rules for Writing Detective Stories” (The American Magazine, 1928) and Ronald Knox’s “Decalogue” (the preface to The Best Detective Stories of 1928–29, 1929) require the writer to present the clues to the reader. Knox also requires the writer to introduce the culprit early. The criminal must not be “anyone whose thoughts the reader has been allowed to follow”, and the detective’s companion, the Watson, must “not conceal any thoughts which pass through his mind”. Agatha Christie’s The Murder of Roger Ackroyd (1926) has been accused of cheating because its murderer narrates the case and defended on the ground that he can conceal the truth without contradicting it (section 6.1).

The codes express a period convention for one genre. Two principles survive outside it. The reader must have had a way to reach the second reading from what the book showed her. When the telling keeps a fact from a reader who shares the mind that knows it, the book must give a reason for the delay (see test 3, Withholder).

2.5 The unreliable narrator and paralipsis

Wayne C. Booth’s The Rhetoric of Fiction (1961) called a narrator reliable “when he speaks for or acts in accordance with the norms of the work” and unreliable “when he does not”; the reader of an unreliable narrator reads past the narration to an implied author’s norms. Gérard Genette’s Narrative Discourse (1972; translated by Jane E. Lewin, 1980) adds two terms for departures from a narration’s chosen focalisation: paralipsis, giving less information than the focalisation would supply, and paralepsis, giving more. When the narration leaves out a fact that its focalisation should disclose, the omission is a paralipsis. Knox’s rule against concealing the Watson’s thoughts is a genre-specific prohibition of one such omission.

Read together, the two theories suggest the teller rule of section 3.6. A reveal may expose the unreliability of a storyteller whose norms differ from the work’s. The rule treats a narration with no declared teller as speaking on the book’s own authority. If a reveal requires that narration to have stated an untruth, the reader has only the book to hold responsible.

2.6 The evidence

The experiments used short fiction or television episodes. Hans Hoeken and Mario van Vliet (Poetics, 2000) varied the telling of one story. Readers gave higher ratings to the version with a surprise than to the comparable version without one. They were also better at recognising sentences about events that preceded the surprise. The authors suggest that readers reassessed those events in light of the surprise. That reassessment would be a second reading. Readers also showed signs of suspense when they already knew the outcome. Jonathan Leavitt and Nicholas Christenfeld (Psychological Science, 2011) found that readers who saw a spoiler paragraph before a short story enjoyed it more. Benjamin Johnson and Judith Rosenbaum (Communication Research, 2015) found the reverse. In their experiment with 412 readers, participants rated stories without spoilers as more suspenseful and more enjoyable. In experiments with short stories and television episodes, Thomas Daniel and Jeffrey Katz (Psychological Reports, 2019) found that spoilers reduced enjoyment under some conditions for television, with no comparable effect for the short stories. The studies give different answers about how foreknowledge affects enjoyment. They leave open the chapter’s conjecture that much of a reveal’s value lies in the second reading it prompts.

3. The extended catalogue of reveals

3.1 The four objects, and the reveal of world

The Core chapter classifies reveals by what they change. The reveal of event discloses what happened. Maxim’s confession in Rebecca (1938) recasts silences the narrator read as grief. The reveal of identity discloses who someone is (Magwitch as Pip’s benefactor in Great Expectations, or the psychologist as one of the dead in The Sixth Sense). The reveal of motive explains an act the reader has already watched a character perform. Okonkwo’s fear supplies an instance at chapter scale (Chapter 6). The father’s illness explains why he kept a record in the household novel. The reveal of telling changes what the reader understands about who authored the text, why it was written or whether it is true (The Murder of Roger Ackroyd, Gone Girl, Atonement).

A fifth object is the reveal of world, which the Core chapter folds into the other four objects. When a reveal discloses the rules of the story’s world, even its ordinary scenes change meaning. Kazuo Ishiguro’s Never Let Me Go (2005) discloses, gradually and then plainly, that its students were created to provide organs. The reader now sees what the institution was preparing the students for, and ordinary scenes of school life take on that purpose. A reveal of world has enough range to recast the book as a whole.

3.2 The gradual reveal

Not every reveal has a moment. In the gradual reveal, each disclosure confirms something the reader has begun to infer, and she comes to her second reading in stages. Never Let Me Go is the model. The reader has half anticipated Miss Lucy’s disclosure to the students, and the book’s interest moves from what is true to how the characters live with it. The reveal can fail when the book puts too much weight on a single disclosure that the earlier increments have already given away. It succeeds when the reader’s inferences and the characters’ acknowledgement advance together.

3.3 The false reveal

A false reveal presents a disclosure as true. A later reveal corrects it. In the legitimate form, the false disclosure comes from a source within the book, such as a lying witness or a character who draws the wrong inference. The reader can then see why the first account seemed true and what the mistake meant. When the narration itself announces a solution and then retracts it, the reader learns to doubt anything the book states. Knives Out (section 6.3) shows the legitimate form.

3.4 The serial reveal

A serial reveal is a chain of disclosures, each recasting the last. The chain works when the reader reaches the end able to see how the disclosures fit together. When each voids the previous one, the reader stops investing in any reading at all (section 4.3).

3.5 The declined reveal: a ruling

Chapter 13 proposed the epistemological domain. A writer grants a viewpoint in this domain to show why no single vantage can establish what happened. Chapter 13 adopted the domain for diagnosis while conceding three objections: that it may amount to contrast plus unreliability, that its examples cluster in modernist fiction, and that it names what the structure means rather than what the writer gains by granting a viewpoint. Chapter 13 left the question open at the scale of a book.

At that scale, the epistemological domain is the plural-vantage form of the declined reveal. The book holds a question of fact open to the end and shows the reader that it is doing so. The ending supplies a test for the first objection. If a reveal tells the reader which account to discount, contrast and unreliability are enough to describe the arrangement. If the accounts conflict and the ending leaves the reader with no grounds to choose between them, their irresolution is part of the book’s design. The second objection stands in part. The form reaches outside modernist fiction: The Affair keeps its lovers’ memories unreconciled in its first television season, and Yann Martel’s Life of Pi (2001) makes a similar refusal through one teller, who offers two accounts and asks which the listener prefers. The third objection falls away at book scale, where the ruling concerns what the whole arrangement comes to mean.

A book can decline a reveal in other ways. In Life of Pi, a single teller gives incompatible accounts. In the household novel, the mother simply declines to say why she changed the locks. In each case the book keeps the question visible while closing its other questions around it, as Chapter 16 requires of the mother’s silence. If the reader can no longer see the question, the thread has been abandoned. Barthes’s jamming is the refusal made explicit in a sentence: the text acknowledges that the answer will not come.

3.6 The teller rule and range

Chapter 6 proposed the recasting close, an ending that changes the meaning of the chapter it closes. If the recasting close asks the reader to discard too much of what she read, it can void the chapter. Chapter 16 ruled that the category stops discriminating at a book’s last line, when every close looks back. The question it leaves is how far a close may recast a whole book, from a parallel that adds a layer of meaning to a revelation that changes what kind of story the reader has read.

A closing reveal can reinterpret or falsify a passage. It reinterprets when it gives the passage a second meaning and the first still describes what the reader was shown. It falsifies when it establishes that what the reader was shown did not happen. The range of a reveal is the set of passages it recasts in either way. Chapter 7 uses reach for a different measure, the distance an anachrony leaps from the narrative’s present. Reinterpretation may extend as far as the double reading holds. Under the teller rule, a reveal may falsify only passages a teller in the book could have shaped. The reveal must identify the teller, and the book must give a reason for the false account. Atonement shows that Briony invented the Balham reunion and gives her motive. Gone Girl exposes Amy’s inventions in her own diary and gives hers. The Usual Suspects places the flashbacks it calls into question within Verbal Kint’s account. The Murder of Roger Ackroyd and The Sixth Sense recast their whole length by reinterpretation alone, the safest way to reach as far as the opening.

A closing reveal can void the book when it retracts what the narration vouched for on its own authority. A late announcement that the story was a dream or a simulation can have this effect. The recasting close needs no separate category at book scale. The double reading and the teller rule determine how much a close can recast. Section 8 considers books that ask the reader to suspend the contract on which the rule rests.

3.7 Summary table

The table shows what each object or position tends to recast and where that kind of reveal can go wrong.

An object and a position can describe the same reveal. Gone Girl has a turn reveal of telling, Atonement a closing reveal of telling, and We Are All Completely Beside Ourselves an early reveal of identity.

4. The extended catalogue of failure patterns

4.1 The five patterns of the Core chapter

The Core chapter gives five failure patterns: the cheat, the giveaway, the reported reveal, the idle reveal and the reveal from outside. Section 9 defines them. Four more patterns extend the catalogue.

4.2 The coy narrator

Symptom. The narration keeps announcing that it is withholding something: if she had known then, it would be years before he understood, had she looked more closely. Readers grow impatient. When the reveal comes, they have anticipated it so often that it lands as a relief.

Mechanism. The coy narrator turns curiosity into irritation by advertising a gap without giving the reader anything to work with. Each announcement renews a promise that the narration will keep only at the end. The repetition also gives away that a reveal is coming.

Remedy. Keep one announcement and make it carry a clue that bears both readings.

Exception. Death, the narrator of Markus Zusak’s The Book Thief (2005), announces deaths in advance as a matter of style. The reader learns what will happen and is left to wonder how. That foreknowledge turns the announcements into a source of dread, much as foreknowledge gives the prologue of Romeo and Juliet its force in Chapter 10, section 8.6.

4.3 The serial reveal that voids

Symptom. The last act stacks twist on twist, each overturning the last, and readers end unsure what was true and uninterested in deciding.

Mechanism. Each reveal in the chain falsifies its predecessor. Once the reader expects every disclosure to be retracted, she stops investing in any of them. The chain repeatedly breaks the teller rule, or gets round it by naming a new teller at every step.

Remedy. Order the chain so that each reveal recasts the last without voiding it. Let each successive reveal recast fewer passages. Cut any reveal that exists only to overturn the one before.

Exception. The Usual Suspects stacks reveals in its last minutes. They converge on the teller’s identity, which gives the viewer a way to make sense of the whole chain.

4.4 The red herring that eats the book

Symptom. A false trail occupies so much of the book that, when it is abandoned, a large part of what the reader invested in turns out to have been for nothing.

Mechanism. A red herring is a passage built to support a false second reading. When it grows into a thread with scenes and characters of its own, giving up the false reading can mean giving up everything that made the thread matter. The reader experiences the reveal as a loss of the book she was reading. The reveal fails test 6 (Consequence) in reverse, making much of the book idle.

Remedy. Keep red herrings at the scale of a passage. If a false trail must be large, give it a second job, such as developing a character’s arc or giving the theme a variation. The trail can still do that work after the reader gives up the false reading.

Exception. In Wilkie Collins’s The Moonstone (1868), suspicion of Rosanna Spearman and Rachel Verinder occupies much of the book. Both trails still make sense after the explanation. Rosanna acted out of love for Franklin Blake, and Rachel kept silent about seeing him take the diamond.

4.5 The withholding viewpoint

Symptom. A viewpoint character turns out to have known the truth all along, and readers who have spent chapters in her mind feel that her thoughts were edited.

Mechanism. The narration follows a mind that knows the fact the book is withholding. That omission is paralipsis. Without a reason for it, the narration is doing what Knox forbids in his rule about the Watson’s thoughts. An omission in close third person is harder to excuse. A first-person narrator may have a motive the reveal can disclose, while in close third person the reader ordinarily has no teller to hold responsible.

Remedy. Show where the character’s attention is, so that the reader can believe the fact never comes to mind in the scenes she shares. A retrospective frame can give the withholding to a teller who has a reason. A shift to another viewpoint can keep the reader out of the scenes where the fact would surface.

Exception. The household prologue (Chapter 10) follows the father on a day when he knows he is ill. The illness goes unnamed. The prologue directs his attention to the till, the boy and the pen. The one sentence that touches his knowledge, he could not have said in case of what, relies on an equivocation (section 7.2). It is the household novel’s nearest approach to the pattern.

5. The diagnostic procedures

The tests use two tables. In the double-reading table, list every passage that bears on one reveal, in book order. Give each passage’s position as a percentage of the book, then enter its first and second readings. In the disclosure map, give each withheld fact a row and give the reader and each major character a column. In each cell, record where the reader or character learns the fact, or write never. Draft both tables before revising.

Test 1, double reading. Mark every passage before the reveal that supports only one reading. A row with only a first reading may be a cheat. Check it against test 2. A row with only a second reading gives the reveal away. Give the passage a first reading or cut it. Then check where the clues fall. If more than half the rows fall in the last fifth of the book, the reader will feel the clues gathering towards a reveal.

Test 2, teller. For each passage the reveal falsifies, write down who could have shaped it. If the only answer is the narration, and the book has declared no teller, rewrite the passage or give it to a teller whom the reveal can identify. Check that the book gives the teller a reason.

Test 3, withholder. Beside each reveal, name what keeps the truth from the reader: a vantage that does not know, a teller with a motive, or an arrangement. If a character does not know, note the reason for her ignorance and where the book shows it. If a teller keeps the fact back, record the motive and where the book discloses it. If the arrangement withholds the fact, name the convention that makes the delay acceptable. If you cannot name a withholder, look for an unmotivated omission in the viewpoint or a cheat.

Test 4, alignment. For each fact in the disclosure map, compare when the reader learns it with when each character does. If the reader learns it first, check that the gap creates suspense. If she learns it with the character, check that the scene shows the character’s recognition. If a character whose mind she shares knew it first, apply test 3.

Test 5, successor. For each reveal before the last fifth of the book, write the question it answers and the question it leaves the reader holding. If the new question is weaker, move the reveal or find the decision it forces.

Test 6, consequence. For each reveal, note which characters act differently because of it and how it changes the meaning of the ending. If you can name neither a change in action nor a change in meaning, the reveal is idle.

Test 7, recognition. Find the scene in which the character learns the truth. Note how she learns it, whether by discovery or by hearing it from someone else. What does she do with the knowledge in the scene?

Test 8, source. Trace each disclosure to its source and mark where the source first appears. If the source first appears in the chapter of the reveal or the one before it, check that the genre’s contract allows a source to arrive that late.

6. Further analyses

6.1 The Murder of Roger Ackroyd (1926): the range of the whole book without a falsehood

Dr James Sheppard, a village doctor, records the investigation into Roger Ackroyd’s murder. His neighbour Hercule Poirot, who has retired to grow vegetable marrows, takes up the case, and Sheppard acts as his companion. Near the close, Poirot names Sheppard as the murderer. In the last chapter, “Apologia”, Sheppard confesses as he prepares to take his own life. He explains that he had begun the manuscript as an account of Poirot’s failure.

The reveal is of identity and of telling at once. It recasts the whole book by reinterpretation. After the body is found, Sheppard writes that he did what little had to be done. His confession identifies that little task: he removed the dictaphone and put the chair back. The line is an equivocation, true under both readings. Sheppard’s motive for withholding the truth becomes visible only at the end, so Core treats the novel as an exception to test 3 (Withholder). Readers who object that Sheppard hides his thoughts can see in the last chapter why he wrote his account that way. In a close-third version with no teller and no reason why the murder never came into Sheppard’s thoughts, the concealment would be the withholding viewpoint of section 4.5.

6.2 The Silent Patient (2019): withholding by arrangement

Theo Faber, the narrator of Alex Michaelides’s thriller, takes a post as a psychotherapist at a secure unit to treat Alicia Berenson, a painter who shot her husband, Gabriel, and has not spoken since. Theo alternates the account of her treatment with the story of discovering his wife Kathy’s affair. The novel also gives passages from Alicia’s diary. The reader takes the two strands of Theo’s life to run together. The affair took place years earlier, and Kathy’s lover was Gabriel. Theo followed Gabriel and was the masked man who held Alicia and Gabriel captive on the night of the shooting. In the last pages, a police inspector reads the entry in which Alicia implicates Theo.

The reveal concerns the telling. The delay depends on how Theo arranges his account. Alternation invites the reader to assume that the two strands run together, though Theo never says they do. The reader can reinterpret the scenes of his marriage without having to discard them. Setting two undated timelines side by side is what lets Theo withhold their relation, an arrangement discussed in Chapter 8, Non-linear and Multi-timeline Architecture. Whether the arrangement plays fair depends on more than whether the passages can be read either way. Core’s account of the double-reading table calls for at least one early passage that bears the second reading clearly enough for the reader to point to it afterwards. Few passages in Theo’s account meet that test.

6.3 Knives Out (2019): a false reveal and a turn of alignment

Rian Johnson’s film opens on the discovery of Harlan Thrombey’s body the morning after his birthday party. Early in the film, the viewer learns something the detective, Benoit Blanc, does not know. Harlan’s nurse, Marta Cabrera, believes she gave him an overdose of morphine. Harlan coached her in an alibi and cut his own throat to protect her. The film also establishes that Marta vomits when she lies. The whodunit becomes a story of suspense about whether the detective will discover Marta’s part in the death. In the last act, Blanc shows that Ransom, Harlan’s grandson, had swapped the contents of the vials. Marta had recognised the medicines from years of handling them and given Harlan the correct doses. Ransom admits what he did to the housekeeper, Fran, after Marta lies that Fran survived.

The early disclosure is a false reveal of the legitimate kind (section 3.3). The flashback shows what Marta did and what she took the labels to mean. Blanc’s later demonstration changes what those images mean. Because the flashback follows Marta’s recollection, the mistake belongs to her interpretation of what she did. The alignment turns twice. At the first disclosure, the viewer moves ahead of Blanc. At the second, the viewer learns the truth with Marta. The first disclosure replaces the question of how Harlan died with the more urgent one of whether Marta will be found out. The second clears her of causing his death, so she keeps the house he left her.

7. The household reveal in full

7.1 The draft

In the draft convergence, given in “The Problem” in Core, Dov says he took the ledger home and read it. He then reports that their father was ill and had been seeing someone since the autumn. Having Dov read the ledger avoids the question of why he would leave those entries unread. It also spends the reveal. Dov learns first and offstage (test 4, Alignment), so his sisters hear a report (test 7, Recognition). The ending’s He had known. He had not told them no longer comes as a discovery.

7.4 The revision

The revision deletes the six sentences in which Dov says what he did with the ledger and what he found. His account of the ledger ends with “It’s all there. From March, it’s mine.” In his chapter of the evening of 9 March, the revision proposes two sentences after he writes the date and the count:

He did not turn back the page, then or in any of the months after. In eleven years he had never read a page of it; the ledger was for writing in, and reading it back had been his father’s business, and he did not start now.

The two sentences show why Dov never reads back, a reason the case in Chapter 13 needed. He thinks of the dead man as his father and takes it that the ledger has a way of being written in. In his mind, reading it back is still his father’s business. This deference also fits Chapter 14’s proposal that Dov keeps the form of his father’s honesty while leaving his own secrets out of the ledger. The reader can see the reason for Dov’s ignorance before the reveal, so the withholder passes test 3 (Withholder). Dov’s line at the convergence then bears a second reading. His father wrote in case anyone ever needs to check, and the son who kept the ledger for four months never checked.

One alternative would have kept the evidence outside Dov’s mind. At the convergence, Elena would see the till receipt she had used to mark her place still sticking out where she left it. The receipt would add a plant to a scene Chapter 12 and Chapter 14 already find crowded. The receipt could still be in place if someone had read on.

7.5 What the revision settles, and what it does not

The proposal gives the book’s largest reveal a withholder and makes clear why the truth reaches the reader and all three siblings at the shared reading. The reader can begin to infer the illness before the siblings discover it. The reading then answers a question of curiosity rather than producing a surprise. The mother’s silence remains visible as a declined reveal. The two sentences for Dov’s chapter await the author’s confirmation.

Five things remain unsolved. The two sentences state Dov’s motive without dramatising it. They also look ahead from 9 March for a second time in a short chapter, which may underline the clue. Beside the doctor’s name, they give the reader enough to infer the illness at about 78 per cent of the book, at some cost to the surprise. The prologue’s equivocation depends on one verb. And the convergence remains crowded for the sisters, so Elena needs time to take in whose hand it is before Nadia arrives for the reading.

8. The limits of the model

The model assumes a reader who reads forwards once and then looks back, and a book whose facts are stable enough to be disclosed. It fits novels of secrets, mysteries and thrillers. It fits the novel of consciousness less well, since what the reader has yet to discover may be a feeling the character has not yet had. The teller rule also assumes that a narration with no declared teller speaks on the book’s authority. Some books unsettle that assumption deliberately. John Fowles’s The French Lieutenant’s Woman (1969) admits the author into the text and offers alternative endings. In the fantastic, the book may give the reader no way to decide between dream and event. In such books, the teller rule describes an expectation the book invites the reader to question.

The model also says little about the rereader, who already knows what the characters have yet to learn. Section 2.6 leaves open what matters on a later reading, when the surprise has passed and the earlier passages can be understood in light of the reveal.

9. Glossary of the chapter’s terms

These terms also appear in the consolidated glossary.

Alignment. Whether the reader learns a withheld fact before, with or after a character.

Closing reveal. A reveal late in a book that leaves no successor question for the book to answer.

Consequence. The property of a reveal that changes what a character does or what the ending means.

Coy narrator. A failure pattern in which the narration keeps advertising a gap without giving the reader clues or partial answers.

Curiosity reveal. A reveal that answers a question the reader knew she had (after Sternberg’s curiosity).

Decoy reading. A first reading supplied by a character with a reason to believe it. The reader can later see why the character’s mistake seemed plausible.

Declined reveal. A book’s refusal, kept visible to its end, to disclose a fact or decide between accounts. The plural-vantage form is Chapter 13’s epistemological domain at book scale.

Disclosure map. A table that shows when each withheld fact reaches the reader and each major character. Each fact has a row, and the reader and each character have a column.

Double reading. The movement from what the reader understands when she first meets a passage to what she understands after a reveal.

Double-reading table. A list in book order of each passage that bears on a reveal, with its position and its first and second readings.

Early reveal. A reveal in the first third of a book, which turns curiosity into consequence.

Equivocation. A sentence true under both the first and the second reading.

False reveal. A disclosure presented as true and later corrected by another.

First reading; second reading. The understanding of a passage before a reveal, and after it.

Giveaway. A failure pattern in which clues give the reveal away. Some clues support only the second reading. Even clues that support both readings can give away the reveal if they cluster too densely.

Gradual reveal. A truth disclosed in increments, each confirming the reader’s inference.

Idle reveal. A failure pattern in which the disclosure changes neither what a character does nor what the ending means.

Range. The set of passages a reveal recasts, by reinterpretation or falsification; distinct from Chapter 7’s reach.

Recognition. A character’s change from ignorance to knowledge (Aristotle’s anagnorisis).

Reported reveal. A failure pattern: the truth delivered as information by a character or the narration, rather than found.

Reveal. A disclosure of a fact that changes the reader’s understanding of what she has already read.

Reveal from outside. A failure pattern: a disclosure whose source lies outside the book’s causal system.

Objects of a reveal. Event, identity, motive, telling and world: what happened, who someone is, why an act was done, what the text itself is, and the rules of the story’s world.

Serial reveal. A chain of reveals, each recasting the last.

Successor question. The question a reveal leaves the reader holding in place of the one it answers.

Surprise reveal. A reveal of a gap the reader did not know existed (after Sternberg’s surprise).

Teller rule. A reveal may falsify only passages that a teller in the book could have shaped. The reveal must identify the teller, and the book must give a reason for the false account.

Cheat. A failure pattern in which the narration vouches on its own authority for a passage that supports only the first reading.

Turn reveal. A reveal near the middle that changes the book’s engine.

Withholder. What keeps a truth from the reader until its disclosure: a vantage that does not know, a teller with a motive, or an arrangement.

Withholding viewpoint. A failure pattern in which the narration follows a mind that knows the truth and gives no reason for keeping it from the reader. See paralipsis.
