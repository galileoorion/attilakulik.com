Chapter 17 · Series and Genre

Series and Genre

Continue in Core

Promises Inherited and Promises Deferred: The Full Treatment

The Craft Behind the Story (Second Edition), Reference — Chapter 17. Part IV: Beyond the Book and Into Practice.

This chapter is new in the second edition. It extends Series and Genre in Core and restates the working definitions in section 1 so that the discussion can be read on its own. Quotations are brief and serve criticism and study. Section 10 lists the sources and identifies the translations used. The analyses disclose the endings of the works they discuss.

1. Scope and working definitions

A genre, for a writer, is the set of expectations a reader brings to a book she has identified as belonging to a group of works treated as alike. Readers recognise a genre by its conventions. Following Rick Altman, this chapter distinguishes semantic elements (places, objects, figures and kinds of event) from syntactic elements (how those elements relate and in what order events happen). The core promise of a genre is the convention whose violation its readers treat as a breach of contract; an obligatory scene is one the genre’s readers expect to find. A mode carries a genre’s character into a work of another kind. The work borrows conventions, takes an adjective such as gothic and usually leaves the genre’s core promise behind. A hybrid makes two core promises and keeps both. A book can keep, vary or decline a convention. In a declared decline, the book signals the decline early enough for the reader to believe it. The book keeps the declined question in view and honours the promise it makes in its place.

A series is a sequence of separately published works that share characters, a world or a continuing story and invite the reader to connect them. Each work is a volume; this book calls a later volume a series instalment and keeps sequel for the reaction, dilemma and decision that follow a scene in Chapter 2. Each volume has a volume question. The form of the series tells the reader how much of that question the volume must close. It may also carry a series question, which the series can close as late as its last volume or leave open. A series hook, in Chapter 16’s sense, leaves a major question open at the end of one volume to draw the reader into the next. The opening of a later volume is its re-entry, which must serve a returning reader and a new one. When a continuing thread disappears for one or more volumes, the gap is its dormancy across volumes.

A genre is a set of promises the reader brings to a book and a series a set she carries out of it. The book keeps faith with both if it keeps the conventions it invokes or signals their decline before the reader has staked much on them. Each volume closes the question or stage its form promises to close. It leaves open only questions that it has shown to belong to the series. A book that declines a genre’s central payment can satisfy that genre’s readers if it tells them in time; a book that pays everything can still fail them if it promised something else.

The boundary that matters most in revision is between a series and a set of books that share a setting. A set of books by one writer in one town, each with its own cast and none carrying a question, is a series only in a bookseller’s sense; it makes no promise across volumes beyond the place and the voice, and the chapter’s series tests do not apply to it. The relay series (section 3.2) lies at the threshold: it carries a world and its people, and if reading in order is rewarding, the series has made the reader a small promise.

The chapter leaves the genre contract of the first pages to Chapter 9, the prologue’s genre test to Chapter 10, the serial’s chapter endings to Chapter 6, the declined reveal to Chapter 15, the last line and the series hook at a book’s end to Chapter 16, and the arrangement of times within a volume to Chapter 8. Market categories and publishing economics are outside its scope.

2. The theory at length

2.1 The horizon of expectations

In his inaugural address at Konstanz in 1967, Hans Robert Jauss argued that a work is received against a horizon of expectations formed by the reader’s experience of earlier works of its kind, its themes and the difference between poetic and practical language. A new work confirms, varies or breaks that horizon, and Jauss measured a work’s aesthetic distance by how far it departs from the horizon. For a novelist the theory’s use is narrower and practical. Genre largely forms the reader’s horizon before she opens the book, and she judges the book against it from the first page. A writer cannot choose that horizon, but she can correct it early. If the book leaves the inherited horizon uncorrected, the reader judges the book by it to the end.

2.2 Kinds and modes

In Kinds of Literature (1982), Alastair Fowler argued that genres are better understood as families than as classes. Members of a family resemble one another without sharing a single defining feature. He distinguished the kind, a genre with its full repertoire including its external form, from the mode. A mode borrows some of the kind’s features without its form and takes an adjective such as comic, pastoral or gothic. The distinction helps a writer ask whether a book that uses a genre’s furniture also takes on its core promise. When the book borrows only the furniture, a reader who expects the core promise has mistaken the mode for the kind. The household case shows how easily that can happen. The novel is a family novel in the mystery mode, and the draft jacket sells it as a mystery.

2.3 The detective story’s two stories

Tzvetan Todorov’s “The Typology of Detective Fiction” (1966; translated by Richard Howard in The Poetics of Prose, 1977) described the classic whodunit as two stories: the story of the crime, complete before the investigation begins and absent from the novel’s action, and the story of the investigation, slight in itself and concerned with recovering what happened. He contrasted the whodunit with the thriller, which fuses the two stories and tells the story of the crime as it happens, so that curiosity gives way to suspense. The account explains why the mystery’s core promise is structural. The investigation story is not complete until the crime story has been told in full, so a mystery that declines its solution has left its own second story unfinished, whatever else it achieves. A family novel in the mystery mode can borrow the investigation without committing to the crime story, provided the book shows that the investigator’s question will go unanswered.

2.4 The romance’s elements

The Romance Writers of America defined the genre by two elements, a central love story and an emotionally satisfying and optimistic ending, and added that a romance may have any tone or setting. Pamela Regis’s A Natural History of the Romance Novel (2003) described the form historically, from Samuel Richardson’s Pamela to the contemporary category romance, and found eight elements in it: a definition of the society the lovers must reform or leave, their meeting, the barrier between them, their attraction, the declaration, the point of ritual death at which the union seems impossible, the recognition that removes the barrier, and the betrothal. Regis’s list shows how much a romance can vary its syntax while keeping its promise. An element can be brief, displaced or ironised, so long as the betrothal or its modern equivalent arrives.

2.5 Formula and the iterative scheme

John Cawelti’s Adventure, Mystery, and Romance: Formula Stories as Art and Popular Culture (1976) defined a formula as a conventional story structure familiar to a culture’s readers, and argued that formula stories balance convention, which gives the pleasure of the known, against invention, which keeps the known from going dead. Umberto Eco’s “The Myth of Superman” (1962; translated by Natalie Chilton, 1972) described the iterative scheme of the serial hero, whose stories each begin as if the last had not happened. Superman never ages or uses himself up, and the reader loses the sense of time progressing. In “Innovation and Repetition: Between Modern and Post-Modern Aesthetics” (1985) Eco argued that serial forms please two readers at once. The naive reader enjoys the story, and the competent reader enjoys the variations on the scheme. Together the two accounts explain the craft of an episodic series. Readers come for the scheme, and the writer decides what each volume can do within it.

2.6 Semantics and syntax

Rick Altman’s “A Semantic/Syntactic Approach to Film Genre” (1984) proposed that critics had defined genres either by lists of elements (the western’s horses, hats and frontier towns) or by structures of meaning (the western’s opposition of wilderness and civilisation), and that both were needed. Genres are most stable when a semantic set and a syntax coincide, and they change when one is carried over without the other. Altman’s later Film/Genre (1999) added that genres are made and remade by producers, critics and audiences using them, so a genre’s boundaries are always under negotiation. For the writer the distinction suggests two common variations. She can give an old syntax a new dress or put an old dress on a new syntax. The dress can also lead readers to expect a syntax the book never uses, a failure this chapter calls the genre in costume (section 4.1). A hybrid can fail by keeping neither genre’s syntax.

2.7 Series and saga

Eco’s essay of 1985 also offered a typology of serial forms: the retake, which continues a successful story; the remake, which retells it; the series, in which a fixed situation and a fixed set of characters recur and each story is complete; the saga, in which the characters age and history accumulates; and intertextual dialogue, in which a work quotes others. Jason Mittell’s Complex TV: The Poetics of Contemporary Television Storytelling (2015) described a mode of television that combines episodic and serial storytelling, closing a story each week while carrying arcs across seasons, and made the viewer’s knowledge of the form part of its pleasure. The first three forms from the chapter’s series catalogue (section 3.2) can be traced to these accounts. The episodic series corresponds to Eco’s series, the running arc uses Mittell’s combination, and the serial tells Eco’s saga as one story.

2.8 The evidence

In two experiments reported in “Effect of Genre Expectations on Text Comprehension” (1994), Rolf Zwaan varied the instructions readers received for the same texts. Those told they were reading literary stories read more slowly and remembered more of the wording and less about the situations described than those told they were reading news. The experiments used short texts and compared two broad categories. The finding the chapter draws on is that what a reader believes she is reading can change how she reads it. How readers of one fiction genre respond to another’s conventions remains a separate question.

3. The extended catalogue

3.1 The five promises in detail

The Core chapter sorts genres by five core promises. For each promise, the catalogue shows which conventions and scenes readers expect and where the writer has room to vary them.

The promise of solution. The mystery poses a question of fact, usually who committed a crime and how, and guarantees an answer the reader can trace to evidence the book has shown her. Its conventions include the investigator, the closed circle of suspects, the clue, the false solution and the fair-play rule that the reader see what the detective sees, codified in Ronald Knox’s “Decalogue” (1929) and S. S. Van Dine’s twenty rules (1928). Its obligatory scenes are the discovery of the crime and the exposure. Its readers accept an unpunished culprit, a detective who fails at everything but the answer, and an exposure performed by someone other than the detective; they do not readily accept an unanswered question, unless the book has declared early that the question will stay open.

The promise of union. The romance guarantees that the lovers at its centre end together and that the ending is emotionally satisfying and optimistic. Its conventions are Regis’s eight elements and, in contemporary category romance, a narrow viewpoint allocation, often alternating between the lovers. Its readers accept a union that is a beginning rather than a marriage, a barrier that is internal, and a point of ritual death that is quiet; they do not accept a death or a parting at the end, which moves a book to another shelf, as Me Before You, the Core chapter’s exception to its test 2, shows.

The promise of the threat met. The thriller, the horror novel and the adventure establish a danger and guarantee its direct confrontation. Their conventions include the clock, the escalation of stakes, the hero at the mercy of the threat and, in the thriller, a threat to many as well as to one. Their readers accept a hero’s death, a pyrrhic victory and a threat that survives in diminished form; they expect the hero and the threat to meet. The Coens’ No Country for Old Men shows how far a film can vary that promise. Moss and Chigurh confront each other, and the film withholds a final reckoning.

The promise of the kept rule. Science fiction and fantasy declare a world unlike ours and guarantee that it will obey its declared rules, so that problems are solved within the world’s terms. The critic Darko Suvin called science fiction’s effect cognitive estrangement. The world is strange enough to be seen freshly and consistent enough for the reader to reason about. The conventions include the novum, the new thing that makes the world differ, and the cost of the rule. Readers accept a rule never explained, and resist one broken to resolve a plot.

The promise of significance. Literary fiction guarantees that its particulars will add up to more than a summary of its events could hold, and that the ending will alter the reader’s view of what preceded it. Its conventions include the open or partial ending, the domestic scale, the close attention to consciousness and the epiphany. Its readers may accept the decline of another genre’s payment. They may read a payment the book never promised as a concession, as when a novel ends with a wedding nobody believed in or a confession that arrives from nowhere to solve a murder.

3.2 Further series forms

The Core chapter names four forms of series: episodic, with a running arc, serial and relay. Two further forms are the anthology and the companion.

The anthology shares a premise, a title or a tone and nothing else: each volume has its own world and cast. Rod Serling’s The Twilight Zone (1959–64) is the classic screen case. It promises a kind of experience, not a continuity, and its volumes are judged as standalone works; its single convention across volumes is its frame, which is a formal contract in Chapter 9’s sense.

The companion is a book set in a series’ world whose question is unrelated to the series question, often centred on a minor character. A companion is a relay volume that makes no promise to the series and asks nothing of it; it must stand as a book.

3.3 Summary table

The table compares the six forms by what each volume closes, what carries over, and what the reader can expect of the next volume.

Form A volume closes What carries What the form promises of the next volume
Episodic The volume question The hero, the world, the hero’s rule The scheme again, varied
Running arc The volume question One line, a step per volume The scheme, and the next step
Serial A stage The story The story continued, in order
Relay The volume question The world and its people A new centre, in a known world
Anthology Everything A premise or a frame The kind of experience again
Companion Everything The world Nothing

Use the last column to check whether a volume changes the contract with the reader. If an episodic volume ends on an open question, or a serial volume closes the story before the series is over, the book should signal the change.

3.4 Prequels and reading order

A prequel is a volume set before the others in story time, usually written after them. At the scale of the series, a prequel read after the later-set volumes is an analepsis in Chapter 8’s sense. If those volumes have already told the reader what happens, the prequel needs a question they leave open. It can ask how, why or at what cost, or follow a character they could not show. The order of reading is then a decision with consequences. C. S. Lewis’s The Magician’s Nephew (1955) was the sixth Narnia book published and comes first in story time. Later editions renumbered the series in story order. A reader who starts there reads a creation story, while a reader following publication order finds answers to questions raised by The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe. Neither order is wrong, but the writer should know which reader she is writing for.

3.5 Genres that expect a dual timeline

Some genres take an arrangement of times as a convention. The dual-timeline mystery braids a present-day investigator, often a woman tracing her family, with a strand in the past, and its readers expect the strands to converge by identity. Someone in the present turns out to descend from, or to be, a person from the past. Kate Morton’s The Forgotten Garden (2008) is an example. In these mysteries the genre gives the writer a reason to braid the strands and makes their convergence an obligatory scene. Chapter 8’s necessity test still asks what this particular arrangement does for the reader. Its strand question test also applies. If the past strand only explains the present, it lacks a question of its own. The time-slip romance promises a union across times, while the multi-generational family saga promises a pattern the youngest generation completes.

4. The extended catalogue of failure patterns

4.1 The five patterns of the Core chapter

The Core chapter treats five patterns, each with its symptom, mechanism, remedy and an exception. The undeclared decline invokes a core promise and declines it at the end without a sign inside the book (exception: No Country for Old Men). The genre in costume uses a genre’s semantic elements without its syntax, so readers apply a promise the book never makes (exception: The Buried Giant). The volume that owes its question leaves its own question open at the last page to bring the reader back (61 Hours shows how to end on a hook while closing the volume question; see section 6.3). The deferred answer holds a series question across volumes without advancing it (exception: the Harry Potter novels and Snape’s allegiance). The recap at the door opens a later volume with summary (exception: the television Previously on sequence). Four more follow.

4.2 The reset hero

Symptom. A series hero changes in one volume and starts the next as she was before. Readers may feel that the previous book did not count. Mechanism. The reset hero changes her criterion in one volume, in Chapter 12‘s sense. The next volume restores her old criterion without a scene to show why. That unshown change fails test 8. The cause is usually the episodic form reasserting itself over a running arc the writer did not mean to start. Remedy. Either show the reversal, as a scene in the new volume with its own price, or move the change in the earlier volume to another character, so that the hero’s criterion was never at stake. Exception. Seinfeld (1989–98), whose writers worked to Larry David’s rule “no hugging, no learning”. Its characters learn nothing because the series declared from the start that they would not; the reset is the form, and the comedy is the characters’ immunity to consequence.

4.3 The undone ending

Symptom. A series ends a character or a story conclusively, and a later volume reverses the ending. Mechanism. An ending gives the reader a way to interpret the volume she has just read. That is Chapter 16’s retroactive lens. An undone ending takes that reading away. Every later ending has less authority, since the reader has learned that this series can revoke an ending. Remedy. Avoid a conclusive ending that may need undoing; if you must undo one, make the reversal cost something in the story and give the earlier ending a reading that survives the reversal. Exception. Arthur Conan Doyle’s “The Final Problem” (1893), in which Holmes and Moriarty go over the Reichenbach Falls, and “The Adventure of the Empty House” (1903), in which Holmes returns. Doyle recalls receiving a letter of protest when he killed Holmes. Readers who wanted Holmes back got a story to account for the return. Holmes explains how he survived the encounter with Moriarty and why he stayed in hiding.

4.4 The escalation ratchet

Symptom. Each volume brings a larger threat than the one before, until the series’ world can no longer contain the threat and the stakes stop meaning anything. Mechanism. The escalation ratchet starts when the writer applies Chapter 11’s rule about raising the price of the remaining choices to a whole series. Each threat must now be larger than the last. The promise of the threat met concerns the confrontation. Raising the scale of each threat eventually reaches a ceiling. Remedy. Raise the cost to the hero. A smaller danger can still raise the stakes if it takes something she cannot spare. Exception. The Harry Potter novels, whose threat grows from an attempt to steal a stone to a war across seven volumes. The series gets away with the ratchet because the growing threat costs Harry people he loves. The stakes rise for Harry as well as for his world.

4.5 The overloaded last volume

Symptom. The final volume of a long series is longer than the others, slower in its last third, and disappoints readers who loved the earlier volumes. Mechanism. The overloaded last volume has only the length of one book in which to close the series question and every thread that still runs through the series. The reader judges the whole series through that ending, as Chapter 16’s account of series endings explains. A deferred answer (section 4.1) can help produce the overload. Remedy. Close carried threads in the volumes before the last, so that the last carries only the series question and the threads that the answer needs. Exception. The Lord of the Rings, whose last volume closes its main question with several chapters still to come and spends them on returns, the scouring of the Shire and departures. A reader may find the return too long or come to love the book for the time it gives to the aftermath.

5. The diagnostic procedures

Test 1, shelf. Write two sentences of jacket copy based only on the first chapter and two sentences based only on the last chapter. If the two versions belong on different shelves, decide which shelf fits the book and revise the end that sends the reader elsewhere.

Test 2, core promise. Name the genre the opening declares and its core promise, from section 3.1. Find where the book keeps that promise. If you cannot find the payment, use test 3 to check whether the book declares the decline.

Test 3, declared decline. Give each convention the book invokes a row in the promise inventory. Record its genre, how far into the text the book first invokes it as a percentage, and where the book keeps, varies or declines it. For each convention the book declines, find the first sign of the decline inside the book. If the sign falls after the first quarter of the book, move it or add an earlier sign. Then name the replacement promise and find where the book keeps it.

Test 4, variation. Mark the convention the book varies most. Read the ending and ask whether the variation carries the book’s meaning there. A formula book may pass this test by declaring its scheme; record the choice.

Test 5, volume question. Write the volume question in a line. Find the scene that closes it and record how far into the volume the scene falls as a percentage. If that scene is missing or belongs to a later volume, this volume owes the reader an answer.

Test 6, series step. Name in a phrase the step each volume takes on any continuing series question. The volume may reveal a fact, remove a possibility or make a character pay a cost. If two successive volumes take no step on that question, the series has stalled.

Test 7, re-entry. Give the first chapter of a later volume to a reader who has not read the series, and ask her who the people are and what they want. Mark every sentence that summarises an earlier volume. Count how many facts the summaries give the reader that she needs to follow the opening scenes.

Test 8, criterion across volumes. Using Chapter 12’s criterion log, write down the rule the hero acts by at the end of the previous volume and at the start of this one. If the two rules differ, find the scene that shows the change.

The volume table gathers tests 5 to 8 in one row per volume. Record the volume question and where it closes, the step on the series question, and which threads carry over. For a dormant thread, count the volumes it misses. Record the hero’s criterion, how the volume brings the reader back into the series, and what the last page closes or leaves open.

6. Further analyses

6.1 Bring Up the Bodies (Hilary Mantel, 2012): the middle volume of a serial with a known end

Mantel’s trilogy opens with a beating Thomas Cromwell suffers as a boy in Putney in 1500. The main story runs from 1527 to his execution in 1540, an end the reader may already know from history. The question is how a man so able comes to fall. Each volume takes a step towards that answer by closing a stage in Cromwell’s career. Wolf Hall (2009) ends with his rise and the death of Thomas More, Bring Up the Bodies with the fall of Anne Boleyn in 1536, and The Mirror & the Light (2020) with Cromwell’s own fall. A middle volume must find an end of its own while the story still has further to run. Mantel makes the middle book the most self-contained of the three volumes. Early on it asks how Cromwell will rid the king of Anne, and her execution closes that question. By bringing about her fall, Cromwell also makes the enemies who will destroy him. The book brings readers back into Cromwell’s story with a scene that begins His children are falling from the sky. The children are falcons named for his dead daughters, and Cromwell is out hawking with the king at Wolf Hall in September 1535. A new reader learns that Cromwell grieves and serves, and a returning reader remembers the daughters Cromwell lost in the first volume, without a line of recap.

6.2 Beach Read (Emily Henry, 2020): a romance about the romance’s promise

Henry’s novel makes genre its subject and keeps its genre’s promise. January Andrews writes romances with happy endings. What she learns about her father after his death makes her stop believing in those endings. Her neighbour for the summer, Augustus Everett, writes acclaimed literary fiction with dark endings. They agree to swap genres, each writing a novel in the other’s. The book’s syntax is Regis’s: the meeting, the attraction, the barrier (each lover’s distrust of the other’s kind of ending as well as of the other), the point of ritual death and the declaration. The variation lies in what the lovers argue about, the promise their own story makes. Their distrust of each other’s kind of ending gives the novel an objection to answer as it makes good on the romance’s promise.

6.3 61 Hours (Lee Child, 2010): a hook in an episodic series

The Reacher novels follow an episodic scheme. Reacher arrives in a place, finds an injustice, ends it and leaves, with his rule unchanged. 61 Hours, the fourteenth volume, sends him to Bolton, South Dakota, in winter, where he agrees to protect Janet Salter, an elderly librarian who is the only witness willing to testify against a methamphetamine operation. The police chief kills Salter. Reacher kills the chief, then kills the head of the operation in an underground facility. He makes for the stairs as fire breaks out in the tunnels. Later, a military officer in Virginia, Susan Turner, sees news reports of the explosion. The book settles the fate of the witness and the operation. The hook concerns what the series carries from one volume to the next, the hero himself. Worth Dying For appeared later the same year with Reacher alive in Nebraska. Fourteen volumes into an episodic series, the reader has reason to expect his return. The hook asks how he survives, and the story this volume owes is already closed.

6.4 Twin Peaks (ABC, 1990–91): a series question answered and not replaced

David Lynch and Mark Frost’s series opens on the body of Laura Palmer and is asks the question that will drive the first season. Who killed her? Through the first season, the show advances that question while adding to the town’s other mysteries. The seventh episode of the second season, “Lonely Souls”, shows Laura’s cousin Maddy being killed by Leland, Laura’s father, under the influence of the spirit called BOB. Two episodes later, in “Arbitrary Law”, Cooper brings the investigation to its answer. The series prepared that answer, and it is terrible. Once the investigation closes, the question that drove the series has no successor of equal force. Give each episode a row in the volume table and the problem becomes visible. There can be no further step on Laura’s case, so what question will the next row advance? A series could instead declare that it will leave its first question open, as The Leftovers does.

7. The household case in full

7.1 The draft

The household novel is a contemporary literary family novel, quiet and domestic in register, set in a small British town with a ring road. Four months after the father dies in March, his three adult children reconstruct what he left. At the first chapter break, Elena learns that their mother changed the shop’s locks, and Dov refuses to say why. The book leaves that question open. The mother never has a viewpoint or gives an account of herself. The writer makes two additions at her agent’s request, to prepare the way for a second book. After Nadia puts on the coat, a new last page has the mother ring and Elena answer. The draft jacket reads In March their father died, and their mother changed the locks. Four months later, one of his children is going to find out why.

7.4 The revision

Version B removes the added last page. The novel ends as it did, with Nadia in the coat and her mother’s silence still in view. The proposed jacket copy promises something the book can deliver. Four months after their father’s death, Elena finds that her key no longer fits the shop, and that someone else has been writing in his ledger. The reader learns whose hand it is in Dov’s chapter, about 78 per cent of the way through the book. The sisters learn at the convergence. The jacket states what happened to the locks. The text signals that their mother’s reason will stay unknown through Dov’s refusal and through three decisions of Elena’s. In the truck she decides to drive to the shop instead of calling her mother. After finding the empty drawer she decides against asking Dov who took the ledger. When Dov brings the ledger back, she calls Nadia so that all three can read it together.

7.5 What the revision settles, and what it does not

The proposed revision leaves a standalone novel with no series question. The genre declaration rests on choices the characters make in the text. Four points remain open. Elena’s choice in the truck is her first sign that she will let the question of the locks stand. A reader may recognise that sign only in retrospect. The signs accumulate, and a reader expecting a mystery may still wait for an answer to the end. The publisher can still put the question back on the jacket. The weekly brushing of the coat remains an undeclared decline at this stage. The writer can reveal who brushes it, signal the decline or cut the detail. Finally, the revision gives the agent no second book.

8. The limits of the model

The model assumes that a reader arrives with one horizon and holds it. A reader can bring several and change her expectations as she reads. The model also assumes that genres have core promises stable enough to name. The Romance Writers of America adopted the optimistic ending as part of its definition of romance, and the detective story’s fair-play rules were written down in the late 1920s. A writer in fifty years may find the five promises out of date. The model attends to the jacket, though readers also meet a book through reviews and recommendations. Some readers move between genres and take pleasure in a book that declines a convention. The model’s use is narrower. It lets the writer see which promises her book makes and keep or decline each on purpose.

9. Glossary of the chapter’s terms

The consolidated glossary collects these definitions.

Anthology. A series whose volumes share a premise, title or frame and nothing else.

Companion. A book set in a series’ world whose question is unrelated to the series question; it makes no promise across volumes.

Convention. A feature readers expect of a genre, semantic or syntactic.

Core promise. The convention that readers take as a condition of a genre’s contract. The five core promises in the model are solution, union, the threat met, the kept rule and significance.

Declared decline. A convention the book signals early that it will decline. The book keeps the declined question in view and keeps the promise it makes in its place.

Declined convention. A convention a book invokes and does not pay.

Deferred answer. The failure of a series that holds its question open across volumes without advancing it.

Dormancy across volumes. The absence of a continuing thread. Measure it in volumes skipped and in the months a reader waits for the thread to return.

Episodic series. A series whose volumes close their own questions and carry forward the hero, the world and the rule the hero lives by.

Formula. A conventional story structure a culture’s readers know (Cawelti).

Genre. For a writer, the set of expectations a reader brings to a book she has identified as one of a group of works treated as alike.

Genre in costume. The failure of a book that uses a genre’s semantic elements without its syntax.

Horizon of expectations. The expectations, formed by earlier works, against which a reader receives a new one (Jauss).

Hybrid. A book that makes two genres’ core promises and keeps both.

Kind. A genre with its full repertoire of features, as distinct from a mode (Fowler).

Mode. A genre’s character in a work of another kind, described by an adjective such as gothic. The work borrows conventions and usually leaves the genre’s core promise behind (Fowler).

Obligatory scene. A scene a genre’s readers expect and miss if it is absent (Coyne).

Paratext. Everything that surrounds and presents a text: title, cover, jacket copy, epigraph, shelf (Genette).

Prequel. A volume set before the others in story time; an analepsis at the scale of a series.

Promise inventory. A table with one row for each convention a book invokes. Record the genre, where the book first invokes the convention, and whether and where the book keeps, varies or declines it.

Re-entry. The opening of a later volume, which must serve a returning reader and a new one.

Recap at the door. The failure of a later volume that opens with summary of the earlier ones.

Relay series. A series that keeps a world and passes the centre to a new protagonist in each volume.

Reset hero. A series hero who changes her criterion in one volume and starts the next with her old criterion, with no scene to show why.

Running arc. A line carried across an episodic series, advancing a step per volume while each volume closes its own question.

Semantic and syntactic elements. A genre’s building blocks, and the relations and order among them (Altman).

Serial. One story published in volumes, each closing a stage.

Series. A sequence of separately published works that share characters, a world or a continuing story and invite the reader to connect them.

Series instalment. A later volume of a series; this book’s term for what is elsewhere called a sequel.

Series question. A question that runs across volumes. The series may close it as late as the last volume or leave it open.

Undeclared decline. The failure of a book that invokes a core promise and declines it at the end without a sign inside the book.

Undone ending. A conclusive ending in one volume reversed by a later one.

Variation. Keeping a genre’s core promise while changing a convention: new semantics on an old syntax, a varied obligatory scene, or a scene performed by someone unexpected.

Volume. One work of a series.

Volume question. The question a volume is built on and must close.

Volume table. A table with one row per volume of a series. Record the volume question and where it closes, how the volume advances the series question, which threads carry over or lie dormant, the hero’s criterion, the re-entry and what the last page closes or leaves open.

Volume that owes its question. The failure of a volume that leaves its own question open at the last page to bring the reader back.
