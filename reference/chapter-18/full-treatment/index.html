The Structural Edit

The Order of Revision: The Full Treatment

The Craft Behind the Story (Second Edition), Reference — Chapter 18. Part IV: Beyond the Book and Into Practice.

This chapter was written for the second edition. It extends The Structural Edit (Chapter 18) in Core and can be read on its own. Section 1 restates the working definitions, and section 10 gives the full citations. Quotations are brief and serve criticism and study. The analyses disclose the endings of the works they discuss.

Contents

  3. The extended catalogue: every chapter’s instruments on the ladder; kinds of structural edit; where the order bends; the ruling on formal closure
  4. The extended catalogue of failure patterns
  5. The diagnostic procedures, with the book map and the edit list

1. Scope and working definitions

A structural edit revises the decisions that span more than one scene in a complete draft. Those decisions govern what the book promises and asks, whose story it is, how its times and threads are arranged, the shape of the whole, and the joins between its units. A line edit takes the scene’s job as given and revises its sentences and paragraphs. A copy-edit checks correctness and the continuity of facts. Susan Bell calls the writer’s own work on the whole draft a macro-edit and her work on the lines a micro-edit.

The book map is a table with one row per scene. It records the scene’s position, story time, place, viewpoint, goal and decision, thread and change. It also records what the reader and characters learn, what the scene deposits, and any promise it invokes. The revision ladder sets the order in which a writer checks and repairs the book’s decisions. It has five rungs, from the promise through allocation and arrangement, the shape, and the joins to the scene and its materials. The ladder records an order of dependence. A decision depends on a higher one if a change higher up can put the lower decision out of place. For example, a change in the order of the book’s times can leave a chapter break in the wrong place. Moving the break leaves the order of times as it was.

Four rules govern the ladder. Repair runs down it, finishing each rung before moving to the next. Diagnosis runs up it by the rung-up rule. Before repairing a failure, check the rung above for its cause. If a failure recurs after repair or appears in several places on the same rung, look higher for the cause. Upward discovery occurs when work on a lower rung shows that a higher decision is wrong. The writer goes back up to remake that decision before coming down again. The stopping rule has three conditions. A pass down the first three rungs changes nothing on rungs 4 and 5, the writer defends every kept failure in a sentence, and a cold read produces notes that do not cluster above rung 5.

The edit list records each note and failing test. It gives the rung where the symptom was felt, the rung of its cause, the repair, and the reopened tests. These are the tests a repair may cause to fail in the units it touches. A final entry records whether the writer mended the failure, kept and defended it, or passed the note to the line edit. To judge the economy of the repair, weigh the notes it answers against the tests it reopens and the words it adds.

The chapter puts the earlier chapters’ tests of the text in revision order and adds seven tests of the edit itself. Section 3.4 puts the form audit on the ladder, following Chapter 16’s proposal on formal closure. Section 8 touches on the working relation between a writer and an editor. The line edit and copy-edit fall outside the chapter’s scope.

2. The theory at length

2.1 Revision research

In a study published in 1980, Nancy Sommers compared how twenty first-year university students and twenty experienced adult writers revised their own essays. The students treated revision chiefly as rewording. They tended to replace or delete words and phrases, an approach that Sommers likened to looking for answers in a thesaurus. The experienced writers described revision as finding the shape of their argument. They added, deleted and reordered material at different scales, working back and forth between the whole essay and its parts as the shape changed. The study describes what writers did in their essays and leaves open which revisions made the essays better. Its students were novices in a genre they had been taught to treat as correct or incorrect. The experienced writers’ search for the shape of an argument is closer to what a writer does in a structural edit.

In 1986 Linda Flower, John R. Hayes, Linda Carey, Karen Schriver and James Stratman proposed a working model of revision. A writer detects a problem, diagnoses its cause and chooses a strategy, such as revising the passage in place or rewriting it from its gist. The distinction between detection and diagnosis matters when the writer receives a note. The note records where a reader noticed the problem, and the writer still has to decide what caused it. The chapter draws an inference from the model. Until the writer diagnoses the cause, she is likely to mend the problem where it was noticed.

2.2 Editorial practice

The publishing sequence of structural (or developmental) edit, line edit and copy-edit follows an order of dependence. The copy-editor can wait while the author decides which chapters to cut. A well-documented structural note is Maxwell Perkins’s letter to F. Scott Fitzgerald of 20 November 1924 on the typescript of The Great Gatsby. Perkins praises the design, objects that Gatsby is somewhat vague, and proposes little touches to suggest Gatsby’s business. In the galleys Fitzgerald moved the history of James Gatz from Chapter VIII to the opening of Chapter VI, a repair discussed in Core 18’s worked analyses. Susan Bell’s The Artful Edit (2007) opens with the Gatsby papers. Bell puts the macro-edit, a reading of the whole draft for structure and proportion, before the micro-edit, which works at the level of the line.

2.3 Scene maps

Shawn Coyne’s The Story Grid (2015) asks a writer to build a spreadsheet with one row per scene. Its columns include the scene number, word count, story event, value shift, polarity shift, turning point, viewpoint, time, duration, location, and characters on and off stage. Coyne also asks the writer to summarise the book on a single sheet, the Foolscap Global Story Grid, in a beginning hook, middle build and ending payoff. Composition teachers use a related instrument, the reverse outline. The writer makes it after drafting and summarises what each unit does. The book map adds columns for the decisions, threads, disclosures, deposits and promises that this book’s other instruments track.

2.4 The situation model and the map

In the event-indexing model of Rolf Zwaan, Mark Langston and Arthur Graesser (1995), readers build a model of a narrative’s situation by tracking five dimensions. These are time, space, protagonist, causality and intentionality. Their study found support for the model in a memory task using simple narratives. The question of what a transition costs the reader of a novel goes beyond that experiment. Chapters 5 and 8 apply the model to transitions and orientation across times in novels. These dimensions inform the map’s columns for story time, place, viewpoint, goals, threads and changes. A structural edit may change how the reader connects one event to the next.

2.5 Readers’ notes

In his rules for writers, published in 2010, Neil Gaiman says a reader who senses that something is wrong is almost always right. In his view, a reader who prescribes a fix is almost always wrong about how to make it. He leaves the repair to the writer. The advice follows the distinction between detection and diagnosis in section 2.1. Perkins, for instance, identified a problem that Fitzgerald addressed in his own way. In the edit list the writer records where a reader noticed a problem, then gives her own diagnosis of its cause.

2.6 Swoopers and bashers

In Timequake (1997), Kurt Vonnegut calls writers who draft a story quickly and then go over it again swoopers. He calls himself a basher, a writer who finishes each sentence before going on to the next. The ladder applies to both because it sets an order of decisions. Even a basher must decide what the book is and where a sentence belongs before she can finish that sentence.

2.7 The evidence and its limits

The studies behind the chapter deal with writers revising essays (Sommers, Flower and her colleagues) and readers making sense of short narratives (Zwaan and his colleagues). The editorial sources supply accounts and advice. The order of dependence comes from putting the models of the earlier chapters together. It inherits their account of a novel built from scenes driven by wants and decisions, whose sequence gives the book its shape. A book whose unit is the voice or the pattern still has an order of dependence, but its rungs hold different decisions, and its sentences may sit higher than this chapter puts them.

3.2 Kinds of structural edit

Where a structural edit starts on the ladder depends on why the draft needs one.

The edit of a discovery draft. A draft written to find the book usually has no settled promise on rung 1. Begin by building the map and writing the two sentences of the promise test. Once the promise is clear, the writer may have to draft scenes again to give them the jobs the book now needs.

The edit on notes. A draft with a settled promise and a set of readers’ notes begins with the edit list, sorted by the rung of each cause. This is the case of Core 18’s household revision.

The edit to a constraint. If a book must lose a fifth of its length or meet a publisher’s limit, begin on rung 2 or 3. Cutting a thread, strand or viewpoint can save more words and reopen fewer tests per word than trimming every scene. Trying to cut a fifth of the book on rung 5 alone is premature polish under another name.

The editor’s letter. In a structural letter, a professional editor usually gives a diagnosis with each note. The writer places each note on the ladder, checks the diagnosis by the rung-up rule, and decides which changes to make. In the Gatsby case Perkins identified concerns that Fitzgerald addressed through changes of his own.

The late edit. An edit in proof can still be structural, as Fitzgerald’s was. Each test it reopens costs more to rerun when the joins and sentences around the change have already been finished.

The edit across a series. While the volumes are still in draft, a writer can move a strand or disclosure between them, as section 6.1 shows. Once an earlier volume is published, a later one can change how readers understand it. Series and Genre (Chapter 17) describes that kind of rereading.

3.3 Where the order bends

The order bends in three places. The decisions on rung 3 depend on one another, so check them together. A change to one reopens the others before it reopens any test on a lower rung. Upward discovery sends the writer back up when work on a lower rung shows that a higher decision is wrong. That is normal in an early edit. In a late edit, it can show that the writer left a decision at the top of the ladder to stand by default. Some writers begin with a last line, image or scene and build the book to it. The ladder treats that choice as part of the promise on rung 1. If the writer begins with a last line, the rest of the edit must make that line answer a question the book has taught the reader to ask.

3.4 The ruling on formal closure

The Ending (Chapter 16) proposed formal closure as a sixth domain of the ending. A book may complete, modify or disrupt the structural pattern it has declared. Chapter 16 adopted the domain for diagnosis while conceding that a pattern can serve the five other domains. It left the ruling to this chapter and gave it two test cases, Nineteen Eighty-Four’s Appendix and the last episode of Six Feet Under.

The ruling. The five domains of the ending stand. At the scale of the whole book, formal closure becomes the form audit, a test on rung 2. The audit takes in every formal pattern the book declares, including the person and tense of the narration, divisions and headings, and any frame, apparatus or repeated shape. For each pattern, ask whether the book completes it, shows a reason for modifying it, or needs to remove its declaration.

The first test case. In Part One, chapter 1 of Nineteen Eighty-Four, a footnote declares the Appendix: Newspeak was the official language of Oceania. For an account of its structure and etymology see Appendix. The Appendix fulfils that promise after the narrative ends. It describes Newspeak in the past tense. One reading takes that tense as a vantage from which the regime is over, against the story’s He loved Big Brother. The tense permits that thematic and emotional reading while leaving the regime’s fate open.

The second test case. Six Feet Under (HBO, 2001–05) opened nearly every episode with a death. Its last episode, “Everyone’s Waiting” (2005), opens with a birth. As Claire drives east in the closing montage, the viewer sees the principal characters die over the following decades. The episode varies the pattern at its opening and completes it at the close. The deaths let the viewer see each life whole.

The reasons. Both endings use a formal pattern to deliver an effect in one of the other five domains. That is why the pattern belongs in an audit of the book’s form. A declared pattern has distinctive failures and remedies, as Chapter 16 argued. If the ending leaves a frame open, for instance, the writer can close the frame there or remove it from the opening. Both repairs address a decision on rung 2. For the ruling in the chapter on endings, see section 4 of Reference Chapter 16 and The Taxonomy in Core Chapter 16.

4. The extended catalogue of failure patterns

4.1 The five patterns of the Core chapter

Core 18 describes five failures of revision, each with a published exception. The page-order edit revises in reading order (Make Me). Premature polish starts the line edit before the top rungs settle (Vonnegut’s description of bashing). The local repair mends a symptom where it was felt when its cause lies higher on the ladder (the second ending of Great Expectations). The cascade leaves the consequences of a repair unchecked (Watson’s wound). The edit by addition answers every note by adding material (A Little Life, whose length is part of its promise). Three further patterns follow.

4.2 The endless edit

Symptom. Each round of revision answers the last round’s notes and produces a new set of comparable size. The writer has revised for years, and early readers prefer an early draft. Mechanism. Without a stopping rule, the edit stays on the lowest rung, where there is always more to do. Each pass moves the book further from the conviction of its first draft while the higher decisions remain unsettled. Remedy. Apply the stopping rule and get a cold read. Check rung 1 as well, since the writer may still need to decide what the book is for. Exception. A book may take years to find its promise. Ishiguro abandoned two attempts at the novel that became Never Let Me Go before he found its governing premise.

4.3 The edit by committee

Symptom. The draft has been revised to answer every reader, and readers now find it reasonable and inert. Mechanism. When the writer adopts part of each reader’s prescription, the compromises carry the draft away from the book she meant to write. Remedy. Record where each reader felt a symptom. Look for symptoms that cluster at one place or on one rung and diagnose their cause before choosing a repair. Exception. A panel of genre readers can test the promise on rung 1. Use Chapter 17’s promise inventory to identify the expectations that the book has invited.

4.4 The rearranged book

Symptom. A draft has been reordered, often more than once, and readers find each version as hard to follow as the last. Mechanism. Rearranging the book changes how the reader places one event in relation to the next. For the change to earn its place on rung 2, it has to serve a decision on rung 1. The necessity test in Non-linear and Multi-timeline Architecture (Chapter 8) asks what the arrangement does that chronology could not. Remedy. Write the question each strand asks before moving any strand. If you can write none, try chronology. Exception. Rearranging the draft can help a writer discover what the order is for. The remix of Arrested Development in section 6.4 gives the viewer two arrangements to compare.

5. The diagnostic procedures

5.1 Building the book map

Build the map from the draft in reading order, with one row per scene. For the map, a scene is a unit without a gap in time or place. A chapter of three scenes therefore takes three rows. To record a scene’s position, divide the word count before it begins by the draft’s total word count and express the result as a percentage. In the change column, compress the two sentences from The Purposes of a Scene (Chapter 1) into one answer. What was true when the scene opened and has ceased to be true by its close? Leave the cell empty if nothing changes. Fill the decision column when a character makes a choice. In the learns column, record facts the plot uses and who learns each one. For a deposit, name the element and give its row number in the plant audit if you have one.

5.2 Keeping the edit list

Give each note a row as it arrives. Record who made the note and where she felt the problem before trying to diagnose it. Use the table in Core 18’s diagnostic tests to place the symptom on the ladder. Check the rung above for the cause and record the rung where you find it. Sort the list by the rung of the cause. Work from the top, listing the tests that each repair will reopen before you make it. Close a row when you have mended the failure, kept it and defended it in a sentence, or passed the note to the line edit.

5.3 The seven tests

Test 1, map. Count the rows with an empty change column. If more than one row in ten is empty in that column, check whether the scenes serve the shape of the book and whether the map came from the draft.

Test 2, promise. With the draft closed, write one sentence for what the book promises and one for the question its choices answer. Read the first and last chapters and list every place where either chapter answers to a different promise or question.

Test 3, rung-up. For each note on the edit list, check that you ran the test on the rung above the symptom and recorded the rung of the cause.

Test 4, reopen. For each repair, list the tests it reopens and the map rows it affects, then check that you reran each test.

Test 5, economy. For each repair, count the notes it answers, the tests it reopens and the words it adds. Flag a repair if the number of reopened tests exceeds the number of notes it answers. Check whether the edit adds more words than it cuts or moves.

Test 6, opening last. Check when you last revised the opening pages and the last page. Both changes should come after the last changes to rungs 1 to 3.

Test 7, stop. Sort the cold reader’s notes by rung. A cluster on a rung above rung 5 shows where the next pass begins unless you can defend the choices the notes question. Every failure you keep needs a defence in a sentence.

6. Further analyses

6.1 The Harry Potter series: a strand moved between volumes

In June 2004, when J. K. Rowling announced the title of her sixth volume, she said she had considered Harry Potter and the Half-Blood Prince as a title for Chamber of Secrets. Information she had first planned for the second book belonged in the sixth. Rowling later clarified that she considered weaving the Half-Blood Prince strand into the first draft of Chamber of Secrets, then removed it entirely before the second draft. She said the strand was separate from the basilisk and Tom Riddle’s diary. The link between volumes 2 and 6 concerned a different discovery Harry made in Chamber of Secrets. The example shows a strand deferred before publication. The diary is a separate example. Its later identification as a Horcrux gives it the kind of second reading described in The Reveal (Chapter 15).

6.2 The Martian (Andy Weir): a page-order composition in public

Weir began publishing The Martian free on his website in 2011, a chapter at a time. At readers’ request he self-published a Kindle edition in 2012. Crown bought the print rights in 2013 and published a revised text in February 2014. Readers had the opening before Weir had written the ending, an exception to test 6 (Opening last). One plausible explanation is that the premise fixed much of the book’s promise, while its chain of problems let later links grow from earlier ones. In addition to correcting scientific and sentence-level errors, the Crown edit removed the original epilogue and rewrote the close. The change in tone shows how much a structural revision can still do after publication in instalments.

6.3 A Little Life (Hanya Yanagihara, 2015): a note kept and defended

In an exchange published in Slate in March 2015, Yanagihara and her editor, Gerry Howard, discussed his concerns about the novel’s length and the suffering piled on its protagonist, Jude. In a later oral history published by the Booker Prizes, Yanagihara’s literary agent and UK editor describe a detailed edit in which she accepted relatively few changes and defended much of the book’s excess. Those concerns reach rung 1 because they ask what experience the book promises its reader. Yanagihara answers the concerns on the same rung by defending the experience she wanted the book to give its reader.

6.4 Arrested Development, season 4 (Netflix, 2013) and its remix (2018)

The fourth season was released as fifteen episodes, each centred on one member of the family and covering an overlapping stretch of the same period. An event seen from one side in an episode might be explained from another in a later episode. In May 2018 Netflix released Season 4 Remix: Fateful Consequences, which recut the material into twenty-two shorter episodes in roughly chronological order, with new narration. A viewer can run the necessity test from Non-linear and Multi-timeline Architecture (Chapter 8) by asking what the first arrangement did that chronology does not. The comparison also shows what a change on rung 2 reopens. The new order needed new joins, which the narration supplies on rung 4.

7. The household case in full

7.1 The draft edit

In Core 18 (The Problem), readers and the agent send eleven notes on the draft. The writer’s first response is Version A, a page-order edit. She adds a sentence announcing Elena’s share of the book. At about 45 per cent of the draft she adds a scene in which Nadia finds the mother in the flat with a clothes brush and leaves without a word. She gives Elena a chapter in which to search the flat and puts a paragraph at the head of Dov’s chapter explaining its date and distance from the present. She cuts the call to Nadia at the convergence and brings Nadia to the shop by chance. At the end she adds a paragraph of Elena’s reflection. She also removes two of the three references to the ledger’s warmth.

7.3 The edit list in full

The edit list in Core 18 (Worked Analyses) records eleven notes. Six call for a structural check, including the question of who brushes the coat, which takes the writer to rung 1. The remaining five notes go to the line edit. One concerns the ledger’s warmth. The writer checks each of the four notes on sentences against a higher rung first.

The note on the prologue’s last sentence sends her to the plant audit on rung 3. She keeps the fragment In case in the prologue and leaves the full line for the memory in Chapter 7’s case. The note on the length of Elena’s sequel in the truck sends her to the joins on rung 4. The sequel and the discovery of the ledger need to stay in the same chapter. The grant keeps Dov’s thoughts from the reader until his own chapter, so she passes the note with his curtness intact. Finally, she checks Nadia’s counting against the shape on rung 3. The habit makes her change legible, so the line edit can thin the lists while keeping the counting.

7.4 The revision in rung order

On rung 1 the writer decides that the weekly brushing of the coat belongs to what the book declines, as the locks do. In the mystery mode the book invokes, the unexplained brushing reads as a clue. The declared decline described in Series and Genre (Chapter 17) gives her a way to show the reader that the book will leave the question open. On rung 2 she keeps Nadia’s scene in the flat in the first chapter, moves the doorway to the first chapter break and gives Dov’s chapter the heading 9 March. The form audit checks how that single dated heading works as an exception, with its date already planted in the ledger. On rung 3 the option list shows the buyer’s deadline holding the stretch between the pen and Dov’s chapter. The disclosure map shows why the convergence feels crowded mainly for the sisters. By then the reader knows that Dov signed the sale papers, at about 58 per cent of the draft, and that the looser handwriting is his, at about 78 per cent. Those are her reasons for keeping the stretch of the middle and the convergence. The convergence does need one repair. It gives Elena’s decision in summary. On rung 4 she rereads the two breaks affected by the rung-2 changes. On rung 5 she writes the exchange that signals the decline, gives Elena’s decision its moment in direct speech, and revises the opening pages last.

7.5 What the revision settles, and what it does not

Version B answers four notes, adds about sixty words, and leaves two kept failures with defences and five notes for the line edit. The proposed sign for the brushing still needs the author’s confirmation. At about 54 per cent of the draft, Nadia says that Dov thinks she brushes the coat, then says she does not. Neither sister asks the next question. Core 18 discusses the cost of that exchange. Dov takes Nadia for the brusher, and Nadia denies it, so the reader may infer that the mother brushes the coat. The ritual may begin to explain the mother, whose reasons are left open. Cutting the weekly brushing from the coat’s first scene would also remove Nadia’s measure of the days between brushings. So far only the writer has defended the two kept failures. Version B still needs a cold read, and the ledger’s three mentions of warmth await the line edit. The plant audit for the whole book belongs to the ending.

8. The limits of the model

The ladder is a model of dependence in one kind of novel, and three limits follow. First, the ladder can show what depends on the book’s promise and governing question. The writer still has to decide what those are. Second, the ladder treats the draft as the result of one writer’s decisions. An editor can make changes that alter what a book means. The question of whose decisions the ladder records remains open. Third, working through the ladder takes time. A writer on a deadline may reasonably revise on the rungs she can reach. She can still use the ladder to tell which repairs will hold and which depend on decisions she has yet to make.

9. Glossary of the chapter’s terms

Appendix A includes these terms in the consolidated glossary.

Allocation and arrangement. Rung 2 of the revision ladder: the viewpoint grant, the threads, the order of times, the prologue and the book’s form.

Book map. A table with one row per scene. It records position, story time, place, viewpoint, goal and decision, thread, change, what the reader and characters learn, deposits and promises. These columns supply the facts that the other chapters’ instruments use.

Cascade. The failure in which a repair changes a fact that other units depend on and the consequence goes unchecked.

Cold read. A reading of a revised draft by a reader who has not read an earlier one, or by the writer after an interval long enough to have forgotten what she meant.

Copy-edit. The check of a text’s correctness and of the continuity of its facts.

Economy of the repair. The notes a repair answers set against the tests it reopens and the words it adds.

Edit by addition. The failure in which the writer answers every note by adding material, so that the draft grows, slows and states what it should show.

Edit by committee. The failure in which the writer partly adopts several readers’ prescriptions and the compromises carry the draft away from the book she meant to write.

Edit list. A record of each note or failing test. It gives the place and rung where the symptom was felt and the rung where the writer found the cause. It also records the repair, the tests the repair reopens and the status of the entry.

Endless edit. An edit with no stopping rule. The writer continues on the lowest rung and moves the book away from its first conviction.

Form audit. The test on rung 2 for every formal pattern the book declares. Ask whether the book completes the pattern, shows a reason for modifying it, or needs to remove its declaration.

Joins. Rung 4 of the revision ladder: chapter breaks, transitions, cutaways and the opening pages.

Line edit. The revision of sentences and paragraphs in a scene that already has a settled job.

Local repair. The failure in which a symptom is mended where it was felt when its cause lies on a higher rung.

Macro-edit; micro-edit. Susan Bell’s terms for the writer’s work on the structure and proportions of the whole draft and on the wording of individual lines, respectively.

Order of dependence. A decision depends on another if a change to the decision it rests on can undo it. The dependence runs one way. Moving a strand can put a chapter break out of place. Moving the break leaves the strand where it was.

Page-order edit. The failure of revising a draft in the order of reading rather than of dependence.

Premature polish. The failure of line editing before the top rungs have settled.

Promise, the. Rung 1 of the revision ladder: the kind of book, its governing question, what it declines, and whether it stands alone.

Rearranged book. The failure of reordering a draft without a reason on rung 1.

Reopened tests. Tests a repair may cause to fail in the units it touches.

Revision ladder. The order in which a book’s decisions are checked and repaired, in five rungs, from the promise to the scene and its materials.

Rung. A level of the revision ladder.

Rung-up rule. Before repairing a failure, check the rung above for its cause. If a failure recurs after repair or appears in several places on the same rung, look higher for the cause.

Scene and its materials, the. Rung 5 of the revision ladder: scenes, sequels, exchanges and details, followed by the line edit.

Shape, the. Rung 3 of the revision ladder: the arc, the middle, the schedule of reveals and the ending, checked together.

Stopping rule. An edit is finished when a pass down the first three rungs changes nothing on rungs 4 and 5, the writer defends every kept failure in a sentence, and a cold read produces notes that do not cluster above rung 5.

Structural edit. The revision of a complete draft at the level of the decisions that span more than one scene.

Upward discovery. The writer finds that a higher decision is wrong while working on a lower rung. She goes back up the ladder to make that decision again before continuing.
