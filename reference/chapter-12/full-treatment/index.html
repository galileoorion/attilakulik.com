Chapter 12 · Arc and Theme

Arc and Theme

Continue in Core

Choice, Change and the Argument of a Book: The Full Treatment

The Craft Behind the Story (Second Edition), Reference — Chapter 12. Part III: The Architecture of the Whole.

This chapter extends Arc and Theme in Core. Section 1 restates the definitions so that this chapter can be read on its own. Illustrative passages are quoted briefly for criticism and study, with sources listed in section 10. Where a work is read in translation, the translator is named.

  3. The extended catalogue of arcs: the disillusionment arc, arcs across an ensemble, the arc held across a series, and a summary table

1. Scope and working definitions

A character’s arc is the course, across a whole book, of the rule by which that character resolves dilemmas. The rule is the criterion, a term from Chapter 2: a rule about which cost is more bearable when both options cost the character something. For most decisions, the character uses a rule she already holds. The hinge, also Chapter 2’s term, is the decision at which a character resolves a dilemma by a new rule. Usually the new rule takes precedence over the old one, which may still matter to the character. To read an arc, set the character’s largest decisions side by side and compare the rules behind them.

A theme is a question a book tests rather than a proposition it asserts, as Chapter 16 puts it for the ending. A topic names what the book is about, and a thesis offers one answer. Each character’s criterion is also an answer to the book’s question. The book argues by setting these answers beside one another and, if the protagonist changes course at a hinge, by showing where her new answer stands among them.

The governing claim is that arc and theme are one structure. A change of criterion takes shape when a decision costs the character something the old rule valued. When the book sets that choice beside the choices of other characters, the decisions argue the theme. Announcing a change or explaining what it means leaves the reader with a description of what the decisions would show. A protagonist who keeps her rule can also carry an arc, provided that she chooses to keep it under pressure.

For the single decision and its dilemma, see Scene and Sequel (Chapter 2). Other chapters take up the allocation of minds through which an arc can be shown from inside (Chapter 13), thematic counterpoint between plot lines (Chapter 14), the completion of arcs and the closure of themes at the ending (Chapter 16), the midpoint’s change of mode or knowledge (Chapter 11), the reader’s change of understanding at a revelation (Chapter 15, The Reveal) or the arc of a character carried across several volumes (Chapter 17, Series and Genre).

2. The theory at length

2.1 Aristotle: character in choice

What shows a reader who a character is? In the Poetics, Aristotle answers that choice reveals character. In S. H. Butcher’s translation: “Character is that which reveals moral purpose, showing what kind of things a man chooses or avoids.” The Greek terms are ēthos, character, and prohairesis, deliberate choice with a view to an end. Aristotle’s ranking of plot above character follows from the definition. A character reveals herself through the choices that make up the plot. Aristotle adds that a speech reveals character only if it shows what the speaker chooses or avoids.

Aristotle’s recognition, anagnorisis, is a change from ignorance to knowledge. The strongest recognitions, for Aristotle, coincide with a reversal of fortune. The arcs in section 3 ask what the character does with the knowledge. In a staged arc, she can act on it at a later hinge. In a refused arc, she comes to understand what the alternative would cost and still chooses by the old rule. The recognition is the climax.

2.2 Forster’s flat and round

E. M. Forster’s Aspects of the Novel (1927) asks which characters can surprise the reader. Flat characters are “constructed round a single idea or quality”; round characters are “capable of surprising in a convincing way”. Forster defends flat characters, which are easy to recognise and remember, and holds that only round characters can sustain tragedy for any length of time. His test of roundness has two halves, surprise and conviction. The seed must prepare the change without spoiling the surprise. The seed, the price of the decision and its enactment then make the change convincing. A surprise without conviction reads as the author’s convenience. A convincing change that always looked inevitable risks reading as a scheduled arc.

Forster’s division separates the capacity to surprise from the need to change. A flat character can change someone else, and a round protagonist can hold to her rule. Neither is a failure in his terms.

2.3 Egri’s premise and its descendants

Lajos Egri’s The Art of Dramatic Writing (1946), written for playwrights, begins from the premise, “a proposition antecedently supposed or proved; a basis of argument”, and gives Macbeth’s as “Ruthless ambition leads to its own destruction”. For Egri, the play proves its premise through characters whom conflict forces to change. Once the writer knows the premise, she knows what kind of person can carry it to its conclusion. Screenwriting teachers took up Egri’s method, and later manuals turned his proof into a sequence of stages.

Robert McKee’s Story (1997) separates characterisation, the observable sum of a person’s traits, from true character, which “is revealed in the choices a human being makes under pressure – the greater the pressure, the deeper the revelation, the truer the choice to the character’s essential nature”. McKee’s controlling idea names the value at stake and the cause by which it is won or lost. He sets it against a counter-idea that the story must give its due.

John Truby’s The Anatomy of Story (2007) gives the hero a weakness and a need, a desire, an opponent and a self-revelation, and calls the story a moral argument, conducted through the hero’s and the opponent’s actions rather than through statement.

K. M. Weiland’s Creating Character Arcs (2016) names five arcs by how a character responds to the lie, the false belief at the start of an arc. In the positive change arc, she gives it up for a truth. In the flat arc, she already holds the truth and changes the world around her. Weiland also names three negative arcs. In disillusionment, the character learns a painful truth. In a fall, she clings to the lie, rejects the truth on offer and comes to believe a worse lie. In corruption, she has known the truth and turns away from it to embrace the lie. James Scott Bell’s mirror moment, discussed in Chapter 11, places a scene near the centre of the book in which the protagonist takes stock of who she has become or wonders whether she can survive what lies ahead.

2.4 The arc models compared

The table sets the main models against the chapter’s terms. A writer moving between manuals can then see where different words describe the same method.

Several of the models make change visible through choice under pressure and prepare the change early. Truby’s self-revelation helps the writer locate a change in knowledge. McKee’s emphasis on choice gives the writer a way to tell when a character has changed her rule. If a book promises a change of rule and stops at self-revelation, the result is an announced arc. Section 8 considers what these tools can tell a writer whose story asks little or no change of its protagonist.

2.5 Theme as a question

Egri’s premise and McKee’s controlling idea both state themes as propositions. For revision, this chapter starts with a question. A proposition written down before drafting tends to produce characters who illustrate it, and the opposition becomes a figure the book exists to defeat. A question can be answered differently by several characters, which gives McKee’s counter-idea and the opposing position in Truby’s moral argument room to take shape. In revision, use the answer map in section 5 to read the characters’ decisions and see what answer the book reaches. A proposition can state that answer.

The Core chapter distinguishes four carriers of theme. The decision at the hinge is the book’s verdict, persuasive because the reader has weighed its cost. In variation, other characters answer the same question differently and so test the verdict. The motif, a recurring object or image, shows that the question persists and where its weight lies but cannot argue alone. Statement, a character or narrator saying what the book means, is the most explicit carrier and the weakest as argument. At the ending it can borrow the authority of what Chapter 16 calls a privileged position. The four carriers run from strongest to weakest as argument according to how directly they let the reader weigh the cost of a choice.

2.6 The evidence

Melanie Green and Timothy Brock (2000) ran four experiments on transportation, the reader’s absorption into a narrative world. Readers who reported greater absorption generally held beliefs closer to those implied by the story and judged its characters more favourably. Labelling a story as fact or as fiction generally made little difference to readers’ absorption or to their beliefs. The experiments support a link between absorption and the beliefs readers held after the story. Whether a statement addressed directly to the reader interrupts that absorption, and whether an enacted theme is more persuasive, are conjectures the experiments leave open.

3. The extended catalogue of arcs

3.1 The five arcs in brief

The Core chapter names five arcs by what happens to the criterion. In the conversion arc the criterion changes once, at a single priced decision, usually late. In the staged arc the rule changes in steps, or the character sees the new rule and acts on it later. In the falling arc a worse rule takes over, usually through decisions the character can defend one by one on the old rule. In the steadfast arc the criterion holds under pressure designed to break it, and the change happens to others. In the refused arc the character sees what the new rule would require and still chooses by the old one.

The Core chapter also rules on Chapter 2’s proposal of the hinge as a seventh beat: at book scale the hinge locates a change of rule. The Core chapter rejects it as a seventh beat because most decisions have no hinge, the reader infers the change by comparing decisions, and the steadfast and refused arcs have no hinge by design. In an arc of erosion, such as Lydgate’s in Middlemarch, the reader may find no single decision where the rule changes.

3.2 The disillusionment arc

Weiland’s disillusionment arc gives the catalogue a sixth form. The character learns that a belief she held is false, and her criterion may survive what she learns or break under it. Those possibilities lead to the refused and staged arcs respectively. Flaubert’s Madame Bovary (1857) is the classic case of a criterion that survives: Emma Bovary’s belief in the romance of novels is disproved by each lover in turn, and she goes on choosing by it until her debts close every move. The disillusionment arc is useful as a diagnosis when a draft’s protagonist learns a great deal and decides nothing differently. If the writer lets the new knowledge change a later decision, she has a staged arc. If the character knowingly keeps choosing by the old rule and the book makes that refusal its subject, she has a refused arc.

3.3 Arcs across an ensemble

A book with several principal characters usually gives them arcs that answer one question in different ways, and the arcs are variations in the sense of section 2.5. Pride and Prejudice gives Darcy a counter-arc to Elizabeth’s: she learns that her judgement was prejudiced, he that his manner was proud, and each person’s change helps the reader see the other’s. The Godfather sets Kay’s arc against Michael’s, and her last position, outside the closing door, is the film’s measure of his fall. The ensemble’s risk is the arc for everyone in section 4.4: if every principal character comes round to the same rule, the variation collapses into a single answer. A character who keeps the old rule would give the reader something against which to measure the change.

3.4 The arc held across a series

When a series relies on a hero whose rule carries over into the next volume, each book can give her a steadfast arc and let the people she meets change. Lee Child’s Jack Reacher is a clear case. In Make Me (2015), a novel Chapter 3 cites for Reacher’s silence, the town changes as he holds to his rule under pressure. Some series give the hero a slow staged arc across volumes, with each book completing a smaller arc of its own. The arrangement is Chapter 17’s subject.

3.5 Summary table

The table summarises the catalogue, including the disillusionment arc, so that a writer can find the form a draft’s protagonist has and the risk that comes with it.

Arc What happens to the criterion Hinge Typical position of the hinge or recognition Main risk Example

Staged Changes in steps, or recognition precedes action One or more Recognition near the middle, action later Spent too fast Emma, Project Hail Mary

Weiland’s positive change arc corresponds to the conversion or staged arc, and her flat arc to the steadfast arc. Both the fall and corruption arcs are falling arcs. The character in a fall begins with a lie, while the character in a corruption arc turns away from a truth she has known. The table keeps Weiland’s name for the disillusionment arc. The refused arc has no place in her scheme.

4. The extended catalogue of failure patterns

4.1 The five patterns of the Core chapter

The announced arc substitutes a realisation for a decision (exception: The Great Gatsby, where the retrospective narrator’s telling is the change). The free hinge changes the criterion after the plot has removed its cost (exception: Jane Eyre, whose return to Rochester is free and whose departure was paid in full). The scheduled arc is predicted from the first chapters and delivered on time (exception: The Kite Runner, section 6.2). The revoked arc returns to the old rule after the hinge because the plot needs it (contested exception: Adventures of Huckleberry Finn. Leo Marx argued in 1953 that the Evasion jeopardises the novel’s significance). The sermon removes variation and leaves the theme to statement (exception: Uncle Tom’s Cabin). Five more patterns extend the catalogue, each with a symptom, mechanism, remedy and exception.

4.2 Replacement

Symptom. The character at the end is a different person: different manner, different wants, different voice. Readers say the change is total and unconvincing.

Mechanism. Without a constant, the reader cannot see how the person at the end grew out of the person at the beginning. Replacement usually comes from revising the end of a draft without revising its beginning, so that two versions of a character meet in one book.

Remedy. Test 5. Keep one thing unaltered and visible in the decision that confirms the change: the want, a habit of attention, a way of speaking.

Exception. Oscar Wilde’s The Picture of Dorian Gray (1890–91), in which the change is moved off the character altogether. Dorian’s face stays young and beautiful while the portrait shows what his acts are making of him. The face is the constant and the painting carries the visible change. The device makes replacement visible as the subject.

4.3 The drifting theme

Symptom. The question the opening poses is not the one the ending answers. Readers find the ending satisfying in itself and unrelated to the book they began.

Mechanism. The writer discovered the book’s real question while drafting, and revised the ending to answer it. The opening still poses the earlier question. The hinge is then a verdict on a question the reader was never asked.

Remedy. Tests 7 and 8. Write down the question the ending answers. Revise the opening to pose it, then use the answer map to see which characters offer different answers.

Exception. Herman Melville’s Moby-Dick (1851), which opens as Ishmael’s account of going to sea to cure his low spirits and becomes Ahab’s tragedy, leaving Ishmael’s own question behind until the epilogue brings him back, floating on a coffin. The shift belongs to the book’s encyclopaedic form, which makes room early for more than one question.

4.4 The arc for everyone

Symptom. Every principal character learns something by the end, usually the same thing.

Mechanism. The variation that should carry the theme collapses into a single answer, and no character holds to an alternative answer. The book becomes a sermon delivered by its whole cast.

Remedy. Test 8. Let at least one principal character keep an alternative answer, at a cost the book acknowledges.

Exception. Most strands in Richard Curtis’s film Love Actually (2003) converge on the answer that love should be declared. Two strands test that answer. Sarah gives up a chance at romance to care for her brother, and Karen stays in a marriage she now knows is damaged.

4.5 The decorative motif

Symptom. Readers notice a recurring object or image and the writer discusses it, yet it never appears at a decision.

Mechanism. The motif carries the theme without arguing it, since it is attached to nothing chosen. It becomes ornament, and the writer, sensing that the theme is not landing, adds more appearances of the motif.

Remedy. Put the motif in the room at the hinge, so that it is present when the character chooses. She might even have to decide what to do with it.

Exception. Virginia Woolf’s Mrs Dalloway (1925), in which the striking of Big Ben marks the hours across many minds without attaching to a single decision. The book is about consciousness in time rather than choice, and the motif carries what the book argues: that the hours are shared and the minds are not.

4.6 The untested steadfast arc

Symptom. The protagonist’s rule holds throughout, and the book never makes holding it cost anything. Readers find the hero admirable and the book inert.

Mechanism. A steadfast arc depends on pressure. Without temptation or loss, the reader never sees what it takes to hold the rule. The reader then has little reason to believe the rule could change anyone else.

Remedy. Test 1, read as a steadfast arc. Offer the protagonist the world’s terms, with something to lose by refusing them.

Exception. Paddington 2 (2017), section 6.3, whose bear is never tempted to be anything but kind. Wrongful imprisonment and a near drowning supply the pressure. The comedy can test his kindness without asking him to consider giving it up.

5. The diagnostic procedures

The eight tests in the Core chapter use two tables.

For a criterion log, take the character’s five to eight largest decisions in book order, one decision per row. For each decision, record its position as a percentage of the book, the options, the choice and the cost. Fill the rule column last. Infer a rule from the choice and its cost, and write it as a short imperative in the character’s idiom.

Position Options Choice Rule Cost

Read down the rule column. Test 1 asks whether the rule changes between the first and last large decisions and whether that result is what the book intends. If the rule changes, test 2 asks which act shows the character deciding by the new rule. For test 3, read the cost column at the hinge and in the earlier rows to see whether the reader has watched the cost grow. For test 4, look for the new rule before the hinge, either in another character’s log or in an earlier scene where the protagonist glimpses the rule and sets it aside. For test 5, compare the scenes behind the first and last decisions for something visibly unchanged. For test 6, look for an earlier situation that returns after the hinge and prompts a different choice.

The answer map starts with a thematic question that admits at least two answers. Give each major character a row. Record the character’s rule from the criterion log, then rewrite it as an answer to the question.

Character Rule Answer to the question

For test 7, check whether at least two decisions in the book give different answers. For test 8, look for a place where the opposition’s answer succeeds or where the protagonist pays a price to reject it. If the protagonist adopts a new rule at the hinge, add that answer to the map. See whether it matches another character’s answer, combines two answers or gives an answer that no character held before.

A last procedure helps with test 6. List the situations the character faces in the first third of the book that could recur. She might leave a call unmade, a question unasked, a door unopened or an object untouched. Any of these situations can confirm a hinge if the character faces it again and chooses differently.

6. Further analyses

6.1 Project Hail Mary: the hinge after the recognition

Andy Weir’s Project Hail Mary (2021) opens as its narrator wakes alone on a spacecraft with no memory of who he is. His memories return in fragments. Ryland Grace, a former scientist turned schoolteacher, believes for most of the book that he volunteered for a mission to save Earth from a microbe dimming the sun. Late in the book a returning memory tells him that he refused out of fear and that the mission organisers sedated him and sent him anyway. Grace recognises the rule he chose by then, self-preservation. His chance to choose differently comes soon afterwards. The organism Grace and Rocky find can save Earth, but Grace realises it will also eat through the fuel aboard Rocky’s ship. Grace sends samples to Earth and gives up his own return to save his friend. In the epilogue, years later on Rocky’s planet, Grace teaches science to children. Weir has said of the late revelation that Grace “resisted the call, but the call overcame his resistance. He didn’t choose to go.” In this staged arc the recognition comes so close to the hinge that the reader learns the old rule’s full cost just as Grace is about to choose by a new one. His work as a teacher is the constant.

6.2 The Kite Runner: the scheduled arc at full price

Khaled Hosseini’s The Kite Runner (2003) is the Core chapter’s exception to the scheduled arc, and it repays a longer look. In the first chapter, set in 2001, Amir recalls a telephone call from his father’s friend Rahim Khan and the words “There is a way to be good again.” The novel then returns to Kabul in the 1970s, where Amir, a wealthy boy, fails to defend his friend and servant Hassan from an assault by Assef, an older boy, and then drives Hassan and his father from the house. The opening already points from Amir’s old wrong towards a chance to put it right. When Amir goes back to Taliban-ruled Kabul to rescue Hassan’s son Sohrab, he finds Assef holding the boy. Assef beats Amir badly, and Sohrab stops him with a slingshot, as Hassan once threatened to do. The symmetries make the change convincing in Forster’s terms even as they give up surprise. What keeps the scheduled arc from failing is the price. Amir used to save himself. At the hinge he goes back for Sohrab at the risk of his life, and the beating makes him pay in his body. The aftermath withholds the cure the design seems to promise. Sohrab stays withdrawn and nearly silent through the last chapters. Even the kite and Hassan’s phrase for you, a thousand times over bring only a small response at the end.

6.3 Paddington 2: the steadfast arc

Paul King’s Paddington 2 (2017) is a steadfast arc on screen. Paddington, a bear from Peru living with the Brown family in London, is wrongly convicted of stealing a pop-up book and sent to prison for ten years. His rule, to be polite and to see the best in people, does not change; the prison does. He offers a marmalade sandwich to the prison’s feared cook, Knuckles McGinty, and the kitchen and its prisoners begin to change. At the climax Knuckles and two other prisoners help Mary Brown rescue Paddington from a sinking brake van. Paddington is cleared. The people he meets have changed because he kept to his rule. As section 4.6 notes, the film tests Paddington’s rule by making him suffer. He goes on being kind. The answer map asks whether kindness can survive an unkind world. The actor Phoenix Buchanan trusts in looking after himself, and his vanity holds as firmly as Paddington’s kindness.

7. The household arcs in full

7.1 The draft

The draft ending, quoted in the Core chapter’s opening, adds two paragraphs after the reading: Elena’s realisation that a family could only know some things together, and Nadia’s explanation, “We protect each other by not saying.” The household canon excludes both paragraphs. The early reader finds that Elena finishes the book as she began it and that the ending tells the reader what to think. The first complaint points to an announced arc, the second to a stated theme.

7.3 The answer map

The question takes up what Chapter 3 asks of the household’s doorway exchange. What do the members of a family owe each other in the way of truth? The map gathers the household novel’s related concerns around this question. The father keeps a record that will outlive him, Nadia counts to keep loss at a distance, and all three siblings have to reckon with what the living owe the dead’s silences.

Character Rule Answer

Three people reading one record answer the question through what they do. The map shows why the father’s answer carries weight. The three can read together because he had kept the ledger alone. The book’s verdict is that he was right to keep the record and wrong to leave the truth for later. The mother’s row leaves her answer open, as the household canon requires. By leaving her reason for changing the locks unresolved, the book limits its verdict to the answers it tests.

7.4 The two passages

The first passage is a mirror moment at about 54 per cent of the book, between Dov’s request at the midpoint and the signing at about 58 per cent. Elena helps Nadia begin to clear the flat and keeps her suspicion of Dov to herself.

“Do you know why he wants it cleared?” Nadia was folding the tea towels into thirds and counting them as she went.

Elena had a box of receipts on her knees and the empty drawer in her head. “No,” she said, which was true.

“You’d tell me, though. If you knew anything.”

“I’d tell you.”

She would remember, later, how easily it had come.

The passage follows Elena’s decision in Chapter 11 against asking Dov who took the ledger. Now her rule makes her do to Nadia what Dov did to her at the doorway. She keeps back part of the truth when her sister asks for it. Nadia’s You’d tell me, though plants the new rule. The second passage gives Elena her hinge at the convergence after Dov’s chapter. He brings the ledger to the shop, where Elena is.

Dov put the ledger on the counter and took his hand off it. “It’s all there,” he said. “From March, it’s mine.” She had wanted it since the drawer. She laid her palm on the cover, and the leather was warm from his arm, and she did not open it.

“Ring Nadia,” she said.

“She doesn’t answer me.”

“She’ll answer me.” She rang, and Nadia answered, and Elena told her to come to the shop, and why.

The reading then follows as it does in Version B of Chapter 16: They read it together, in the end — Elena at the register, Nadia on the stool, Dov standing because he said he’d rather.

7.5 What the revision settles, and what it does not

Chapter 16’s case asks for an act from Elena at the convergence. By ringing Nadia and telling her why she should come, Elena makes the decision that leads to They read it together, in the end. Both scenes are proposed additions to the household canon, recorded as C22 for the author to confirm. In this revision, the illness entries still reach all three siblings together at the ending, as C10 requires.

Five things remain unsolved. The convergence now carries Elena’s hinge as well as the four discharges Chapter 14 counts, and the load may make the reader feel the architecture. Nadia has let Dov’s calls ring out, so her immediate answer to Elena may read as arranged. Elena’s new rule stops at the three siblings. Leaving their mother’s silence unresolved, as the canon requires, also leaves open whether Elena’s rule would extend to her. The mirror moment’s last sentence tells us Elena will remember what she said. This may read as the narration underlining her answer. Finally, this is the third time the ledger feels warm, after its appearances in Chapters 6 and 13. A third mention may tip an echo into a tic, and the sentence survives without the warmth.

8. The limits of the model

The model works best when a character’s story turns on change. Three kinds of book fit it less readily. The picaresque and its descendants send a hero through a world that changes around her without requiring her to change. The criterion log may then report a steadfast arc without pressure. Much modernist fiction observes a consciousness rather than trying it, and the decisions the log needs may not be on the page at all, since the book’s interest is in perception. In long-running series that depend on a hero whose rule survives, the arc may be suspended or spread across volumes. In each case the tools have little change of rule to record, and the exceptions in the catalogue show what such books put in the arc’s place: a constant that is itself the subject, a recognition that is the climax, or a world that changes instead.

A second limit concerns the theme. The answer map assumes that a book’s question can be stated in a sentence and that its characters’ rules are answers to it. Some books are built on several questions that do not reduce to one, and some on a question the writer cannot state outright without giving away part of the book’s effect. The map is still useful in such books, as a way of seeing which answers are present, but its verdict on whether the theme is argued should be weighed against the reader’s experience rather than taken as final.

9. Glossary of the chapter’s terms

These definitions also appear in the consolidated glossary.

Announced arc. A failure pattern: a change reported as a realisation rather than shown in a decision.

Answer map. A table recording the book’s thematic question and each major character’s criterion rewritten as an answer.

Arc. The course, across a whole book, of the rule by which a character resolves dilemmas.

Characterisation. The observable sum of a character’s traits, as distinct from the character revealed in choice (McKee).

Constant. A feature of a character that stays visibly unchanged and lets the reader measure what changes (from Chapter 16).

Controlling idea. McKee’s term for a theme stated as a value and the cause by which it is won or lost.

Conversion arc. An arc in which the criterion changes once, at a single priced decision.

Criterion log. A record of a character’s largest decisions in book order, giving the position, options, choice, rule and cost for each.

Decision (carrier of theme). The choice at the hinge, read as the book’s verdict on its question.

Enactment. Confirmation of a new criterion when a character faces a situation the book has shown before and chooses differently.

Falling arc. An arc in which a worse criterion takes over, usually through decisions the character can defend one by one on the old rule.

Flat and round characters. Forster’s terms: a character “constructed round a single idea or quality”, and one “capable of surprising in a convincing way”.

Free hinge. A failure pattern: a change of criterion made after the plot has removed its cost.

Lie, the. Weiland’s term for the false belief a character holds at the start of an arc.

Mirror moment. Bell’s term for a moment near the centre of a novel when the protagonist takes stock of who she has become or wonders whether she can survive. In an arc, it can show her what the old criterion costs.

Moral argument. Truby’s term for a story’s argument about how to live, conducted through the actions of the hero and the opponent.

Motif. A recurring object, image or phrase that carries a theme by returning in changed contexts.

Premise. Egri’s term for a theme stated as a proposition the play proves.

Recognition. Aristotle’s anagnorisis: a change from ignorance to knowledge, which may precede a hinge.

Refused arc. An arc in which the character sees what the new rule would require and still chooses by the old one.

Revoked arc. A failure pattern: a return to the old criterion after the hinge, because the plot needs it.

Scheduled arc. A failure pattern: a change predicted from the first chapters and delivered on time.

Seed. The appearance of the new criterion before the hinge, usually in another character.

Self-revelation. Truby’s term for the moment the hero sees the truth about herself.

Sermon. A failure pattern: a theme answered one way by every character and left to statement.

Staged arc. An arc in which the criterion changes in steps, or is recognised at one point and acted on at another.

Statement (carrier of theme). A character or narrator saying what the book means.

Steadfast arc. An arc in which the protagonist’s criterion holds under pressure and the change happens to others.

Theme. A question a book tests rather than a proposition it asserts.

Transportation. Green and Brock’s term for a reader’s absorption into a narrative world.

Variation (carrier of theme). Other characters answering the book’s question differently.

Want and need. A character pursues her want consciously. In the manuals her need is what she must change about herself. In this chapter it is a criterion she does not yet hold.
