Appendix G — Genre Conventions

Continue in Reference

Series and Genre (Chapter 17) sorts the genres a novelist most often works in by their core promise, the payment a reader is guaranteed at the end, because that is what a book must keep or visibly decline. This appendix takes the genres one at a time. It covers ten genres, eleven of their sub-genres and three modes that cross them, and for each it sets out what a writer revising in that genre needs to check: the promise and the obligatory scenes its readers will look for, the building blocks and plot relations the genre is known by, the conventions its readers expect, and published works that keep, vary or decline them. A writer who has declared her genre can read its entry against her draft to see which expected scenes are missing and which conventions she has declined, and then put each decline to test 3 (Declared decline) of Series and Genre.

The conventions describe recurring reader expectations and craft choices, not rules every work must obey, and the labels overlap: a book can be a historical mystery in the satiric mode.

How to read an entry

Each entry opens with a sentence of definition. The core promise follows, with the genre’s obligatory scenes and, where there is one, the convention its readers most often accept being declined while the promise is kept. Then come the genre’s semantic elements (its places, objects, figures and kinds of event) and its syntactic elements (the relations among them: who wants what of whom, and the order in which things happen). A list of conventions, a note on series form where one form is characteristic, and two or three examples complete the entry. The terms are those of Series and Genre, defined in its Definitions and Taxonomy and collected in the glossary. The Taxonomy also compares the four series forms the entries name: the episodic series, the episodic series with a running arc, the serial and the relay.

The arrangement follows the three terms of Alastair Fowler’s Kinds of Literature (1982). A kind is a genre in full, with its whole repertoire of conventions and its core promise. A sub-genre narrows a kind by subject or setting and keeps the kind’s promise; an italic note under its heading names the kind and says where else its books are shelved. A mode carries a selection of a kind’s conventions into a book of another kind without, by default, its core promise: a family novel can be written in the mystery mode and never say who did it. Allegory, comedy and satire are treated as modes, in a section of their own after the genres.

Genre Conventions

Romance

Romance makes the development of a central love relationship the story’s main arc and its emotional payoff.

Core promise: union. The lovers end together. The Romance Writers of America define a romance by two elements, a central love story and an emotionally satisfying and optimistic ending. Pamela Regis’s A Natural History of the Romance Novel (2003) finds eight elements in the form: the society defined, the meeting, the barrier, the attraction, the declaration, the point of ritual death, the recognition and the betrothal. The obligatory scenes are the meeting, the declaration and, between them, the point of ritual death, where the union seems impossible. The convention most often declined while the promise is kept is the wedding: the union may be a beginning.

Semantic elements. The meet-cute or first meeting; the shared space that forces proximity (a workplace, a small town, a house party, a road trip); the rival or ex; the confidant; the grand gesture; and, in sub-genres, the ballroom (Regency), the ranch or the fae court (romantasy).

Syntactic elements. Two people are kept apart by a barrier, external or internal, that falls; each partner’s growing knowledge of the other drives the plot; desire and resistance alternate until a crisis makes the union seem lost; one or both partners change enough to remove the barrier, and the declaration follows. Point of view is often shared or alternated between the lovers.

Conventions.

  • A sustained relationship. The protagonists’ growing knowledge of one another drives the main plot. A love story that waits while other events carry the book is a subplot, however large.
    Example. One Day visits Emma and Dexter on the same date, 15 July, year after year, so that each chapter measures how much more they know of each other.
  • Attraction and resistance. Desire meets incompatible goals, fears, obligations or circumstances. The resistance has to weigh as much as the desire, or the reader sees no reason for the lovers to wait.
    Example. In Wuthering Heights, Catherine loves Heathcliff but marries Edgar Linton, because marrying Heathcliff would lower her in the world.
  • Increasing vulnerability. Each partner must revise an assumption or risk an honest choice. The barrier falls because the lovers change, not because circumstances move it aside.
    Example. In Emma, Mr Knightley’s rebuke after Emma insults Miss Bates at Box Hill begins her revision of herself, and she understands that she loves him when Harriet hopes to marry him.
  • A relationship crisis. A rupture tests whether intimacy can survive the central obstacle. It is the point of ritual death in Regis’s account, where the union seems impossible.
    Example. In Normal People, Connell is too embarrassed to ask Marianne whether he can stay with her for the summer and says instead that she will probably want to see other people; she takes it as his wish, and they separate.
  • An emotionally affirmative ending. The union is secured or clearly hopeful.
    Example. Bridget Jones’s Diary ends with Bridget and Mark Darcy together at the close of her year, a union begun rather than settled.

A novel can contain a love story without making the relationship its primary promise.

Series forms. Romance series are most often relays: connected stand-alones that pass the centre to a new couple, one of whom is often a secondary character from the previous book, as in Julia Quinn’s Bridgerton novels.

Examples.

  • Pride and Prejudice (Jane Austen, 1813). Regis uses it as a model case, and every element is visible: Meryton society is defined; Elizabeth and Darcy meet at the assembly and dislike each other; the barrier is pride on one side and prejudice on the other, made concrete in Darcy’s refused first proposal at Hunsford. Darcy’s letter begins Elizabeth’s recognition; Lydia’s elopement is the point of ritual death, when the scandal seems to end any union; Darcy’s secret rescue of the family and his second proposal deliver the declaration and betrothal.
  • Jane Eyre (Charlotte Brontë, 1847). The barrier is literal and hidden: Rochester’s living wife, revealed at the altar, stops the wedding at the point the union seemed secure. Jane’s flight and near-death on the moor are the point of ritual death. The novel keeps the union while varying its terms: Rochester is blinded and maimed, Jane returns independent and with an inheritance, and the final chapter’s famous opening, “Reader, I married him”, delivers the declaration in Jane’s own voice, with Jane as the active party.
  • Me Before You (Jojo Moyes, 2012) shows the promise declined. It uses romance’s semantics (a carer and the man she cares for, proximity, a growing attachment) but Will chooses an assisted death at the end. Many readers who expected a romance felt the ending as a breach. Series and Genre (Chapter 17) cites it as the exception to test 2 (Core promise): a book with a romance’s conventions that keeps the emotional payment and declines the union. It is better described as a love story than as a romance in the RWA’s sense.

Literary fiction

Literary fiction is a broad category that foregrounds character, consciousness, language and form, and is judged by what its particulars add up to.

Core promise: significance. Series and Genre (Chapter 17) assigns literary fiction a core promise of its own: its particulars will add up to more than a summary of events could hold, and the ending will change how the reader sees what came before, whether or not it pays any other genre’s promise. Its obligatory scene is the recognition, in the sense The Reveal (Chapter 15) uses: the moment a character or the reader sees what the book has been about. Its readers accept the decline of any other genre’s payment, and resist an ending that pays a promise the book had not made, such as a wedding nobody believed in.

Semantic elements. The ordinary or domestic setting; the single consciousness or small cast; the remembered past; the symbolic object or recurring image; the epiphany; the ordinary day.

Syntactic elements. Events matter for how they are perceived and reinterpreted rather than for what happens next; a character’s understanding moves from a partial to a fuller view, sometimes too late to act on; meaning accumulates through pattern and juxtaposition; the ending settles an emotional or intellectual arc without necessarily resolving the external situation.

Conventions.

  • Sustained interiority. Perception, memory and thought shape how events are understood. Much of what happens in such a book happens in a character’s mind.
    Example. The first part of To the Lighthouse covers one evening, mostly through what Mrs Ramsay and her guests think during it, while “Time Passes” reports ten years, her death among them, in a few pages.
  • Complex motives. Characters resist simple moral classification. The book asks for understanding rather than a verdict.
    Example. In The Great Gatsby, Gatsby’s fortune comes from crime and serves a romantic dream, and Nick, who reports both, disapproves of him and admires him to the end.
  • Attention to language. Style and point of view contribute meaning beyond the sequence of events. Told in a plainer style, the same events would mean something different.
    Example. In Lolita, Humbert’s elaborate, witty prose is his means of winning the reader’s sympathy, and a reader who notices how it works sees the case against him.
  • Thematic patterning. Images, relationships and choices develop questions that may remain open.
    Example. The stories in Dubliners return again and again to people who cannot leave or change, and “The Dead” closes the collection with snow falling on the living and the dead alike.
  • Flexible closure. An ending may settle an emotional or intellectual arc without resolving every external problem.
    Example. Gilead ends with John Ames blessing Jack Boughton as Jack leaves town: the old man’s resentment is settled, and nothing in Jack’s life is.

Literary fiction can also contain a mystery, romance, speculative premise or other genre plot, making a hybrid if it keeps that genre’s promise as well.

Examples.

  • Mrs Dalloway (Virginia Woolf, 1925). The book confines itself to one June day in 1923 and moves between consciousnesses rather than through plot. Its recognition is engineered by structure: Clarissa never meets Septimus Smith, the shell-shocked veteran whose suicide is reported at her party, yet on hearing of it she understands something about her own choices. The novel’s events add up to more than their summary, a party given and a death reported, which is the promise of significance kept.
  • The Remains of the Day (Kazuo Ishiguro, 1989). Stevens’s first-person narration of a motoring trip is reliable on facts and unreliable on meaning, so the reader’s recognition runs ahead of his. The obligatory scene is delayed to the end: after meeting Miss Kenton, now Mrs Benn, Stevens admits to a stranger on Weymouth pier that he gave his best to Lord Darlington and wasted it. The external situation is unchanged; the reader’s view of every earlier chapter is changed.

Coming-of-age fiction

Sub-genre of literary fiction. The coming-of-age story also runs through young adult fiction and other genres; it is listed here because its tradition, the German Bildungsroman, is a literary one.

Coming-of-age fiction centres a young person’s movement towards a more complex understanding of self and world.

Core promise. None of its own. In literary fiction it makes the promise of significance, with the protagonist’s changed self-understanding as the recognition; in young adult romance or fantasy it carries those genres’ promises.

Semantic elements. The school, the family home and the first place away from it; the mentor and the false mentor; the friend who goes further; the first job, love or loss; rites of passage such as the dance, the exam or the funeral.

Syntactic elements. A threshold disrupts childhood assumptions; the protagonist tests competing models of adulthood; a consequential mistake or disillusionment follows; the ending marks a changed self-understanding, often narrated retrospectively by an older self.

Conventions.

  • A formative threshold. A change in family, school, work, friendship or place disrupts childhood assumptions.
    Example. In Room, the threshold is literal: five-year-old Jack escapes the room where he was born and must learn that the world outside is real.
  • New choices and responsibilities. The protagonist gains freedom while discovering its costs.
    Example. On the river in Adventures of Huckleberry Finn, Huck is free of his father and the widow, and that freedom brings him to the choice to help Jim escape, which he believes will damn him.
  • Conflicting models of adulthood. Parents, peers, mentors and institutions offer competing values. The protagonist often has to reject more than one of them before choosing.
    Example. In The Prime of Miss Jean Brodie, the girls are offered Miss Brodie’s romantic, self-dramatising model against the school’s dull respectability, and Sandy, the most perceptive of them, ends by betraying her.
  • Consequential mistakes. Experiment, embarrassment, betrayal and loss become sources of learning. The mistake has to cost something lasting, or nothing is learned from it.
    Example. In Atonement, thirteen-year-old Briony’s false accusation sends Robbie to prison, and she spends the rest of her life trying to atone for it.
  • A changed self-understanding. The ending marks growth or disillusionment, even if adulthood remains unfinished.
    Example. The Kite Runner ends with Amir, now an adult, running a kite for Sohrab, the nephew he has rescued, as Hassan once ran kites for him.

The arc depends on the protagonist’s interpretation of events, not simply on the passage of years.

Examples.

  • Great Expectations (Charles Dickens, 1861). The competing models of adulthood are embodied: Joe Gargery’s loyal decency against the gentility Pip learns from Miss Havisham and Estella. The formative mistake is structural, because Pip believes Miss Havisham is his benefactor; the revelation that the convict Magwitch is his benefactor forces Pip to revalue everything he has become. The older Pip narrates, so the novel’s voice already contains the self-understanding the plot earns.
  • The Catcher in the Rye (J. D. Salinger, 1951). The book inverts the convention of growth: Holden Caulfield’s three days in New York are a refusal of the adulthood he calls phony, and his fantasy of catching children before they fall over a cliff states the refusal as an image. The threshold is passed at the carousel, when he lets his sister Phoebe reach for the gold ring without stopping her, accepting that children must be allowed to risk falling. Holden narrates from a rest home, so the changed self-understanding is partial and undetermined, as the convention allows.

Experimental and postmodern literature

Sub-genre of literary fiction.

Experimental literature tests familiar narrative forms; postmodern writing often makes those tests part of its inquiry into knowledge, representation and authorship.

Core promise. Significance, as in literary fiction, with the form itself made part of what the particulars add up to. These books decline conventional syntax openly and early, which is why their readers take the decline as design.

Semantic elements. The found manuscript, the footnote and the editor’s note; the second-person “you”; the author as character; typographical devices such as the blank page or the diagram; fragments, lists and documents; parodied genre furniture.

Syntactic elements. The relation that matters is between the text and the reader: the book interrupts, recurses, contradicts itself or asks to be assembled; chronology and perspective are multiplied so that no single account governs; the ending often returns the reader to the act of reading.

Conventions.

  • Conspicuous form. Structure, typography, voice or sequence draws attention to how the work is made.
    Example. Tristram Shandy marks the parson Yorick’s death with a black page and later prints a marbled one, so the physical book becomes part of the telling.
  • Disrupted chronology or perspective. Fragments, recursion, contradictions or multiple accounts unsettle a single authoritative version. The disruption changes what the reader can know, and when.
    Example. Time’s Arrow narrates a Nazi doctor’s life backwards, so that at Auschwitz the killing appears to its narrator as healing, and the reader must do the moral reckoning the narrator cannot.
  • Self-reflexivity. The story may comment on its own telling or on other stories.
    Example. Slaughterhouse-Five opens with a chapter on the author’s years of failing to write about Dresden, and he appears later in the story as one of the prisoners.
  • Genre mixing and parody. Familiar conventions are combined, exposed or revised.
    Example. Cloud Atlas nests six stories in six genres, among them a sea journal, a thriller and a dystopian interrogation, breaking the first five off midway and finishing them in reverse order.
  • Active interpretation. Readers must assemble connections that the work leaves open.
    Example. Lincoln in the Bardo builds much of its history from short quoted accounts that contradict one another, down to whether there was a moon on the night of the Lincolns’ reception, and leaves the reader to weigh them.

Formal disruption is most effective when it changes what readers can know or feel, rather than serving as decoration.

Examples.

  • If on a Winter’s Night a Traveller (Italo Calvino, 1979; translated by William Weaver, 1981). Numbered chapters addressed to “you”, the Reader, alternate with the openings of ten different novels, each broken off at its moment of suspense. By making and breaking ten such openings, among them a thriller’s, a romance’s and a political novel’s, the book exposes the promises a genre’s opening makes. Its frame then pays a genre promise ironically: the Reader and the Other Reader, Ludmilla, end married, a romance’s union delivered by a postmodern novel.
  • House of Leaves (Mark Z. Danielewski, 2000). The semantic elements are horror’s, chief among them a house that is larger inside than outside, but they arrive through layered documents: Zampanò’s academic study of a film that may not exist, annotated by Johnny Truant’s footnotes. Typography enacts the house: pages narrow, invert and scatter as the explorers descend. The form is part of what the particulars add up to, which is significance rather than horror’s threat met.

Family saga and domestic fiction

Sub-genre of literary fiction. Commercial family sagas are also shelved with historical fiction or with romance.

Family sagas and domestic novels use kinship, household life and intimate obligations as major sources of plot and meaning.

Core promise. None of its own. Literary family novels make the promise of significance; commercial sagas often promise endurance across generations, or a union, for which they borrow the romance’s syntax. The household novel is the invented draft this book follows from chapter to chapter, summarised in Appendix D. Series and Genre (Chapter 17) finds it to be a family novel in the mystery mode, borrowing a mystery’s investigator, changed locks and an altered document without promising the answer.

Semantic elements. The family house and the family business; the inheritance, the will and the family heirloom; the holiday gathering; the funeral and the wedding; the letter or ledger from a dead parent; the black sheep and the dutiful child.

Syntactic elements. One person’s decision alters the options available to relatives; a private history surfaces and reorganises present loyalties; values shift across generations; the ending revises who belongs and what is forgiven. The saga’s order is chronological across decades; the domestic novel’s is often a compressed present with the past arriving through memory.

Conventions.

  • Interdependent lives. One person’s decision alters the options available to relatives or partners.
    Example. In Everything I Never Told You, Marilyn’s abandoned medical ambitions become the weight her daughter Lydia carries, and Lydia’s death opens the novel.
  • Private histories. Inheritance, secrets, migration, estrangement or past harm shapes present conflict. The present generation may be fighting over the past without knowing it.
    Example. In Absalom, Absalom!, Thomas Sutpen’s concealed first marriage, and the son it produced, return to destroy the dynasty he builds in Mississippi.
  • Generational change. Values and circumstances shift across parents, children and sometimes several decades.
    Example. In White Teeth, Samad Iqbal sends one of his twin sons to Bangladesh for a traditional upbringing; that son returns an Anglicised atheist devoted to science, while the twin who stayed in London turns to militant Islam.
  • The household as a social world. Class, gender, labour, care and money become visible in daily routines.
    Example. In The Grapes of Wrath, the family truck becomes the Joads’ household on the road west, and authority within it passes from Pa to Ma as the old roles fail.
  • Revised belonging. The ending changes who is included, what is forgiven or how the family understands itself.
    Example. Small Things Like These ends with Bill Furlong bringing a girl from the convent’s laundry home to his family, unsure what it will cost them.

A family saga usually spans generations; domestic fiction may stay within a shorter period or a smaller circle.

Examples.

  • Buddenbrooks (Thomas Mann, 1901). Subtitled The Decline of a Family, it follows four generations of a Lübeck merchant house. The saga’s syntax is used against itself: each generation is less fitted to the business than the one before, Thomas exhausts himself keeping up appearances, and the last heir, the musical Hanno, dies of typhoid as a boy. The inheritance that sagas usually pass on is the thing that fails to pass.
  • The Corrections (Jonathan Franzen, 2001). The domestic novel’s gathering is the engine: Enid Lambert wants her three adult children home for one last Christmas in St. Jude while her husband Alfred declines with Parkinson’s disease. Each child’s separate plot is pulled back towards the household, and the gathering, when it happens, revises who belongs and what can be forgiven rather than restoring the family.

Magical realism

A mode rather than a full genre, usually published and shelved as literary fiction; listed as a sub-genre of literary fiction for that reason.

Magical realism introduces extraordinary events into an otherwise recognisable social world and narrates them as part of its ordinary reality. The art critic Franz Roh used the term in 1925; Alejo Carpentier’s related idea of lo real maravilloso (1949) tied it to Latin American history.

Core promise. Significance, not the kept rule. Unlike fantasy, magical realism does not undertake that its marvels obey declared rules, and a reader who expects fantasy’s rule-bound world may read the unexplained marvels as inconsistency. The extraordinary serves what the book’s particulars add up to, often a history that official accounts suppress.

Semantic elements. The village, town or household rendered with social and historical specificity; the ghost, the levitation or the plague of insomnia; folklore and family legend; the political massacre or colonial company.

Syntactic elements. The marvellous and the mundane are given equal narrative weight and the same matter-of-fact voice; characters respond to the impossible as to weather; the extraordinary carries the social or historical meaning the realistic surface cannot state directly.

Conventions.

  • An everyday setting. Family, work, history or community life remains concrete and socially specific.
    Example. The Underground Railroad makes the railroad a real train beneath the ground, but the plantations, towns and slave catchers it connects are rendered with historical specificity.
  • Unexplained marvels. Impossible events are accepted without a system of magical rules. This is the difference from fantasy, whose marvels obey declared rules.
    Example. In Song of Solomon, Pilate was born without a navel, and the novel treats the fact as part of who she is, never as a puzzle to be solved.
  • A matter-of-fact voice. The narration gives the marvellous and the mundane comparable weight.
    Example. When Orlando wakes as a woman in Orlando, the narrator states the change plainly and observes that in every other respect Orlando remains as before.
  • Layered reality. Folklore, memory, belief and history can coexist with ordinary observation.
    Example. In Things Fall Apart, Ezinma is an ogbanje, a child who dies and returns, and the digging up of the stone that binds her to the spirit world is told as plainly as a wrestling match or a harvest.
  • Social consequence. The extraordinary changes how people understand power, loss, identity or collective experience.
    Example. In Midnight’s Children, the gifts of the children born in India’s first hour of independence are destroyed in the Emergency, when they are rounded up and sterilised, so the marvel carries the country’s political history.

The mode has many traditions; treating any culture’s beliefs as mere decorative magic can flatten what those beliefs mean.

Examples.

  • One Hundred Years of Solitude (Gabriel García Márquez, 1967; translated by Gregory Rabassa, 1970). Remedios the Beauty ascends to heaven while folding sheets, and the household’s chief complaint is the loss of the sheets: the marvel is narrated with domestic flatness. The mode’s social purpose shows in the banana-company massacre, a real historical atrocity, which the town’s official memory then erases, so that the impossible erasure is the book’s most realistic event.
  • Beloved (Toni Morrison, 1987). Sethe’s house at 124 Bluestone Road is haunted by the baby she killed rather than see returned to slavery, and the ghost becomes flesh as a young woman named Beloved. No rule explains her; the haunting is the form slavery’s memory takes. The ending is communal, as the convention of social consequence predicts: the women of the town gather to drive Beloved out.

Thriller fiction

Thrillers make sustained tension under an active, escalating threat their central promise.

Core promise: the threat met. A danger to the protagonist, and often to many others, is established and will be confronted directly by the end. The reader is guaranteed the confrontation, not the protagonist’s survival. The obligatory scene is the hero at the mercy of the threat. The conventions most often declined while the promise is kept are the hero’s survival and a clean victory.

Semantic elements. The ticking clock or deadline; the weapon, bomb, virus or stolen file; the villain’s lair and the public place where the attack will fall; the agency, police force or corporation; the chase, the trap and the hidden camera; the unreliable spouse in the psychological thriller.

Syntactic elements. The antagonist acts first and keeps acting; the protagonist reacts, then seizes the initiative; reversals change who holds the advantage; the stakes widen from one person to many; everything converges on a single confrontation, usually preceded by the protagonist’s lowest point at the antagonist’s mercy. Suspense comes from what could happen next rather than from what happened before.

Conventions.

  • Immediate danger. A person, organisation, system or condition can cause serious harm.
    Example. In Jaws, the danger is a great white shark feeding off a resort town, and the town’s need for its summer visitors keeps the beaches open.
  • A proactive opposition. The threat advances while the protagonist acts. An antagonist who waits to be found turns a thriller towards a mystery.
    Example. In No Country for Old Men, Anton Chigurh follows Llewelyn Moss by a tracking device hidden in the stolen money, and keeps coming whatever Moss does.
  • Time pressure. Deadlines or rapidly closing options compress decisions.
    Example. The Da Vinci Code runs over roughly a day after the curator’s murder, with Robert Langdon wanted by the police and hunted by a killer while he solves the puzzles the curator left.
  • Reversals. New information changes who has the advantage and what must happen next.
    Example. In The Girl on the Train, Rachel’s returning memories show that the man she trusted is the killer, and that he had told her she did things she did not do.
  • A decisive confrontation. The ending tests the protagonist against the threat the story established.
    Example. The Silence of the Lambs ends with Clarice Starling alone in the killer’s dark basement, where he can see her through night-vision goggles and she cannot see him.

A thriller may contain a mystery, but its main momentum comes from what could happen next.

Series forms. The episodic series with a steadfast hero is the thriller’s characteristic form: Lee Child’s Jack Reacher arrives, the town changes, Reacher does not.

Examples.

  • The Day of the Jackal (Frederick Forsyth, 1971). Every reader knows de Gaulle was not assassinated, so the book cannot run on whether the threat succeeds. It runs on the clock instead, cutting between the Jackal’s meticulous preparation and Commissaire Lebel’s pursuit, and on how the confrontation will happen. The promise is kept in the final scene on Liberation Day in Paris, where the shot misses because de Gaulle bends to kiss a veteran and Lebel reaches the room in time.
  • Gone Girl (Gillian Flynn, 2012). The psychological thriller’s semantics are domestic: a missing wife, a diary, a husband under suspicion. The midpoint reversal reveals that Amy is alive and has fabricated the diary to frame Nick, which turns the reader’s sympathies and the direction of threat. The confrontation happens, but the book declines the clean victory: Nick stays in the marriage, trapped by Amy’s pregnancy, and the threat survives in the home.

Espionage fiction

Sub-genre of thriller fiction. Some espionage novels, such as mole hunts, use the syntax of crime and mystery fiction instead.

Espionage fiction follows attempts to acquire, protect or manipulate information under political or institutional pressure.

Core promise. Usually the threat met, inherited from the thriller. A mole-hunt novel makes the promise of solution instead: it poses a question of fact, who the traitor is, and guarantees an answer from evidence shown.

Semantic elements. The agent, the handler and the double agent; the safe house, the dead drop and the border crossing; the cover identity; the file, the cipher and the microfilm; the intelligence service’s headquarters.

Syntactic elements. Information, not territory, is what the sides fight over; each side acts on incomplete, planted or misread intelligence; loyalties are tested by the institution as much as by the enemy; the climax reveals what a secret was worth and who pays for it.

Conventions.

  • Hidden objectives. Missions, allegiances and sources are partly concealed.
    Example. In Kim, Kim travels across India as the disciple of a Tibetan lama while carrying messages for British intelligence, and both roles are real to him.
  • Competing intelligence. Characters act on incomplete, planted or misread information.
    Example. In Our Man in Havana, the vacuum-cleaner salesman Wormold invents his agents and sends London drawings of vacuum-cleaner parts as secret installations in the mountains, and London believes him.
  • Tradecraft and risk. Surveillance, cover identities, recruitment and secure communication shape choices.
    Example. In The Thirty-Nine Steps, Richard Hannay survives on a dead agent’s coded notebook and a series of disguises, a road-mender’s among them, while the police and enemy agents both hunt him.
  • Institutional conflict. Agencies and governments can be as threatening as individual opponents.
    Example. In Slow Horses, the failed agents exiled to Slough House find that the kidnapping they are drawn into was arranged from inside their own service’s headquarters.
  • Compromised loyalties. Duty, ideology, personal attachment and survival pull against one another.
    Example. In Tinker Tailor Soldier Spy, the mole is Bill Haydon, who was Jim Prideaux’s closest friend and the lover of Smiley’s wife.
  • A costly disclosure. The climax reveals what a secret is worth and who pays for it.
    Example. In The Secret Agent, when Winnie Verloc learns that her brother died carrying her husband’s bomb, she kills her husband.

Examples.

  • Casino Royale (Ian Fleming, 1953). The book’s syntax is the thriller’s: Bond must bankrupt the Soviet agent Le Chiffre at baccarat, is captured and tortured, and is saved when a SMERSH assassin kills Le Chiffre. The espionage convention of compromised loyalty arrives at the end: Vesper Lynd, with whom Bond has fallen in love, has been a double agent, and her suicide turns a won mission into a costly disclosure.
  • The Spy Who Came in from the Cold (John le Carré, 1963). Alec Leamas’s apparent defection is an operation to destroy the East German officer Mundt, but the final reversal shows that London’s real aim was to protect Mundt, its own asset, at the cost of Leamas and Liz Gold. Institutional conflict becomes the main threat; the confrontation is met at the Berlin Wall, where Liz is shot and Leamas chooses to climb back down to die with her.

Fantasy

Fantasy makes magic, mythic forces or supernatural laws real within the story world.

Core promise: the kept rule. A world that differs from ours is declared, and the book guarantees it will obey the rules it has declared, so that problems are solved within the world’s terms rather than by a new power arriving to solve them. The obligatory scene is the one in which the world’s rule is seen to cost someone something. The convention most often declined while the promise is kept is an explanation of where the rule came from.

Semantic elements. The secondary world and its map; the magic system, the artefact of power and the true name; the dragon, the elf and the god; the chosen one, the mentor and the dark lord; the court, the school of magic and the ruined city.

Syntactic elements. A threat or lack disturbs the world’s order; a protagonist leaves home and acquires knowledge, allies and power under the world’s constraints; power carries a cost the protagonist must pay; the climax resolves the conflict within the rules, often at a personal price that changes the protagonist’s identity.

Conventions.

  • An altered order of reality. The world contains powers or beings that ordinary empirical explanation cannot account for. The difference may be a whole invented world or a hidden part of ours.
    Example. In Harry Potter and the Philosopher’s Stone, the wizarding world exists inside ordinary Britain, unseen by it, and Harry learns on his eleventh birthday that he belongs to it.
  • Coherent consequences. Magic affects institutions, daily life, conflict and the characters’ options. The magic shows in who holds power and how ordinary people live, not only in battles.
    Example. In The Fifth Season, people who can control seismic energy are trained and governed by an institution, the Fulcrum, and the whole society’s lore is organised around surviving the catastrophes its world produces.
  • Wonder and danger. Encounters with the marvellous can inspire awe, desire or fear.
    Example. In Fourth Wing, the dragons that bond with the cadets of a war college are the book’s chief marvel, and they also kill cadets they judge unworthy.
  • Costs and limits. Power becomes dramatically useful when it carries constraints or obligations.
    Example. In Harry Potter and the Deathly Hallows, each of Voldemort’s Horcruxes required a murder and split his soul, and each is a weakness that Harry must find and destroy.
  • Transformation. Journeys and trials often change a character’s identity, loyalties or understanding of power.
    Example. In The Hobbit, Bilbo, who joined Thorin’s company as a reluctant hired burglar, ends the journey by giving away the Arkenstone to force a peace, against Thorin’s wishes.

Fantasy can be set in an invented world or one that resembles our own; a quest is common but not required.

Series forms. Epic fantasy is usually a serial, one story in several volumes read in order; urban fantasy more often runs as an episodic series with a running arc.

Examples.

  • The Lord of the Rings (J. R. R. Tolkien, 1954–55). The declared rule is that the Ring corrupts whoever uses it and cannot be used for good, which is why Gandalf refuses it and why the quest is to destroy rather than wield it. The book keeps the rule at its climax: Frodo, at the Cracks of Doom, cannot give the Ring up, and it is destroyed only because Gollum bites it from his finger and falls. The rule’s cost is seen in Frodo, who is wounded beyond healing and must leave Middle-earth.
  • A Wizard of Earthsea (Ursula K. Le Guin, 1968). Magic works through knowing the true names of things, and the book declares this early at the school on Roke. Ged’s pride releases a shadow that pursues him, and the climax resolves the conflict entirely within the declared rule: Ged names the shadow with his own name, accepting it as part of himself.

Crime and mystery fiction

Crime fiction examines transgression and its consequences; the mystery, its largest branch, centres an unanswered question of fact.

Core promise: solution (mystery). The book poses a question of fact, usually who committed a crime and how, and guarantees an answer from evidence the reader has been shown. The fair-play rules that make this a contract were codified by S. S. Van Dine’s twenty rules (1928) and Ronald Knox’s “Decalogue” (1929). The obligatory scenes are the discovery of the crime and the exposure, where the answer is stated and the evidence is seen to support it. The conventions most often declined while the promise is kept are the culprit’s punishment and the detective’s survival. Crime fiction outside the mystery, such as the inverted crime novel and noir, need not make this promise.

Semantic elements. The body and the scene of the crime; the closed circle of suspects (the country house, the train, the village); the detective, the sidekick and the police; the clue, the alibi and the false lead; the interrogation room; the gathering of suspects.

Syntactic elements. Tzvetan Todorov’s account in “The Typology of Detective Fiction” (1966) describes two stories: the crime, finished before the book begins, and the investigation, whose task is to recover it. The investigator gathers evidence from competing accounts; a false solution is reached and overturned; the exposure reassembles the first story from evidence already shown.

Conventions.

  • A concealed event or truth. A crime, disappearance, secret or suspicious occurrence creates the central question.
    Example. Big Little Lies opens on a death at a school trivia night but withholds who died, so the question of fact covers the victim as well as the culprit.
  • Clues and testimony. Information is distributed so readers can form and revise explanations. The reader sees what the detective sees, and fair play depends on it.
    Example. In A Study in Scarlet, the police read the word RACHE written in blood as an unfinished “Rachel”; Holmes reads it as the German for revenge.
  • Investigation. Observation, inference, interviewing or confrontation moves the plot.
    Example. Each episode of Columbo shows the murder first, so the interest lies wholly in the investigation, carried by Columbo’s questions to the murderer.
  • Competing accounts. Witnesses, records and suspects offer partial or misleading versions of the past.
    Example. The Moonstone is told by several narrators, each of whom knows only part of what happened, and the solution depends on putting their accounts together.
  • A consequential answer. The ending answers the question of fact; it may complicate what justice requires.
    Example. At the end of Murder on the Orient Express, Poirot gives two solutions and lets the false one stand for the police, because the true one shows that the victim was himself a murderer.

Series forms. The episodic series is the mystery’s traditional form (Christie’s Poirot); many modern crime series add a running arc in the detective’s life; Tana French’s Dublin Murder Squad is a relay.

Examples.

  • The Murder of Roger Ackroyd (Agatha Christie, 1926). The book keeps the promise of solution while breaking a convention its readers assumed was fixed: the narrator, Dr Sheppard, is the murderer. Every clue is present in his account, so the fair-play rule is technically honoured through what he omits. The book also declines the culprit’s punishment: Poirot lets Sheppard take his own life to spare his sister the scandal, which is permitted because the promise was the answer, not the arrest.
  • In the Woods (Tana French, 2007). Two questions run side by side: the murder of Katy Devlin, twenty years on, and the 1984 disappearance of the narrator’s two childhood friends, after which he was found alone and with no memory. The book answers the first in full and leaves the second unanswered. The worked analysis in Series and Genre (Chapter 17) treats this as a decline visible enough for many readers to accept, while others felt it as a breach.
  • The Talented Mr Ripley (Patricia Highsmith, 1955) is crime fiction without the mystery’s promise. The reader watches Tom Ripley murder Dickie Greenleaf and assume his identity, so there is no question of fact to answer; the suspense is whether he will be caught, and he is not.

Historical fiction

Historical fiction places invented events or characters within a documented past whose conditions shape the story.

Core promise. None of its own: historical fiction is defined by its semantic elements, and it takes its core promise from the genre whose syntax it borrows, making a historical romance, a historical mystery or a literary historical novel. Its readers do hold one near-contractual expectation, that the documented record is not contradicted without signal, which is why the author’s note explaining departures from fact is a convention of the genre.

Semantic elements. The documented period and its material life (clothes, food, tools, travel); the real historical figure seen at a distance or up close; the recorded event (a battle, a trial, a coronation, a plague); period language; the author’s note.

Syntactic elements. Characters pursue their goals within the possibilities and limits of their time; fictional lives intersect recorded events; the reader’s knowledge of what happened next works as dramatic irony against characters who cannot know it.

Conventions.

  • A specific period. Material life, institutions, beliefs and social rules are more than period decoration.
    Example. Middlemarch is set in 1829–32, and the Reform Bill, the coming railway and new ideas in medicine all bear on what its characters attempt.
  • Historically constrained choices. Characters pursue goals through the possibilities and limits of their time.
    Example. In A Gentleman in Moscow, a Bolshevik tribunal sentences Count Rostov in 1922 to spend the rest of his life in a hotel, and the novel finds his freedom within its walls.
  • Research made experiential. Details appear through action, labour, conflict and perception.
    Example. In Girl with a Pearl Earring, the reader learns how Vermeer’s paints were made through Griet’s work grinding them in his attic.
  • The known and the invented. Fictional lives may intersect with recorded events without making every event a lesson.
    Example. In Vanity Fair, the Battle of Waterloo happens offstage, and the novel reports its effect on its invented characters in a single sentence that leaves George Osborne dead.
  • A situated perspective. The narrative recognises that people in the past did not know what came next. Dramatic irony comes from the reader’s hindsight, which the characters must not share.
    Example. Hamnet never names the boy’s father, so the household lives without knowing what the reader knows, that he will become Shakespeare.

The craft challenge is to make historical conditions causally important while keeping the characters’ concerns immediate.

Examples.

  • Wolf Hall (Hilary Mantel, 2009). The reader knows Anne Boleyn will fall and Cromwell will be executed, so the novel uses that knowledge as irony rather than suspense. It keeps the situated perspective by staying in close third person, mostly present tense, inside Cromwell, so that every choice is made without hindsight. It is the first volume of a serial, the Cromwell trilogy, whose series question, how Cromwell falls, is answered by history before the first page.
  • The Name of the Rose (Umberto Eco, 1980; translated by William Weaver, 1983). A historical mystery, it makes the promise of solution in a Benedictine abbey in 1327. William of Baskerville identifies the culprit, the blind monk Jorge of Burgos, who was once librarian and still controls the library, and finds the hidden book, Aristotle’s lost treatise on comedy. He reaches both by a false pattern, however: the deaths did not follow the Apocalypse as he deduced. The novel keeps the promise of the answer while undermining the detective’s method, a variation that suits its medieval argument about signs and knowledge.

War fiction

Sub-genre of historical fiction. Most war fiction is set in a documented past; some is written from recent experience by veterans.

War fiction explores armed conflict through combatants, civilians, institutions or the societies that sustain war.

Core promise. None of its own. Combat novels often make the promise of the threat met; anti-war and literary war novels make the promise of significance.

Semantic elements. The front, the trench, the base and the occupied town; the unit, the officer and the recruit; the weapon, the letter home and the casualty list; the battle, the leave and the return.

Syntactic elements. Collective decisions constrain individual lives; orders, duty, refusal and survival collide; the unit stands in for the family and its losses for the cost of the war; the ending carries the war’s aftermath into a life or a society.

Conventions.

  • Conflict beyond one person. Political decisions and collective violence constrain individual lives.
    Example. In The Book Thief, a war decided far away reaches Himmel Street as rationing, conscription and finally an air raid that kills nearly everyone Liesel loves.
  • Authority under strain. Orders, duty, refusal and survival collide.
    Example. In Henry V, the king, disguised on the night before Agincourt, is told by a common soldier that if the cause is bad, the king must answer for every man killed in it.
  • Material pressure. Terrain, logistics, injury, exhaustion and uncertainty shape action.
    Example. Dunkirk cuts between a week on the beach, a day at sea and an hour in the air, and the hour is measured by the fuel left in a Spitfire.
  • A contrast of scales. Private loyalties are tested by large campaigns or historical change.
    Example. In Saving Private Ryan, eight men are sent across Normandy to find one paratrooper whose three brothers have been killed, so an invasion is weighed against one family’s loss.
  • Lasting aftermath. Loss, displacement, trauma or altered belief continues beyond a battle.
    Example. In Mrs Dalloway, Septimus Smith still sees his dead officer five years after the war, and his suicide is reported at a London party.

A war novel may be an action story, a historical novel, a family saga or an anti-war work; combat scenes are not required.

Examples.

  • All Quiet on the Western Front (Erich Maria Remarque, 1929; first English translation by A. W. Wheen, 1929). Paul Bäumer’s unit is the book’s family, and its members die one by one. The book declines the combat novel’s threat met: there is no decisive battle, and Paul is killed in October 1918 on a day so quiet that the army report records nothing to report. The anti-war argument is carried by that ironic ending rather than by any statement.
  • The Things They Carried (Tim O’Brien, 1990). Linked stories about a platoon in Vietnam are narrated by a character named Tim O’Brien, who openly invents some of what he tells. The book makes truth a subject: it distinguishes what happened from the story that conveys how it felt, and argues that the second can be truer. The conventions of material pressure and lasting aftermath are literal in the title story’s inventory of what each soldier carries.

Science fiction

Science fiction explores the consequences of altered material conditions framed through science, technology or systematic extrapolation.

Core promise: the kept rule. As in fantasy, a world unlike ours is declared and the book guarantees it will obey its declared rules. The critic Darko Suvin called science fiction’s effect cognitive estrangement: a world strange enough to be seen freshly and consistent enough to be reasoned about, organised around a novum, the new thing that makes it differ. The obligatory scene is the one in which the rule is seen to cost someone something. Readers accept a rule never explained; they resist one broken to resolve a plot.

Semantic elements. The spaceship, the colony and the alien planet; the robot, the artificial intelligence and the clone; the laboratory and the experiment; the future city; the alien; the time machine.

Syntactic elements. A premise, the novum, is introduced; its consequences ripple from the individual to institutions; characters test explanations and adapt to unintended effects; the problem is solved, or fails to be solved, within the premise’s logic; personal choices connect to larger futures or systems.

Conventions.

  • A consequential premise. A discovery, invention, environment or social change reorganises what characters can do. This premise is the novum, and the plot’s problems and their solutions come from it.
    Example. In Never Let Me Go, cloning for organ donation fixes how the students’ lives will end, and the novel follows what they try to do within that limit.
  • Discoverable causality. The altered world operates through intelligible processes, even when its science is imagined. The processes need not be real or explained, but they must be consistent enough for a character or reader to work them out. This is where science fiction parts from fantasy, whose powers ordinary empirical explanation cannot account for.
    Example. The sun-eating microbe in Project Hail Mary is invented, but Ryland Grace works out its properties by experiment, and the book never lets it break them.
  • Ripple effects. The premise changes institutions, ethics, work and ordinary life, not only the spectacular scenes.
    Example. On Anarres in The Dispossessed, the abolition of property reaches into the language, which avoids possessive pronouns, and into the work postings that send partners to different parts of the planet.
  • Inquiry and adaptation. Characters test explanations and respond to unintended consequences. This is how discoverable causality becomes scenes rather than exposition. The unintended consequences bring complications from the rule itself, which lead to the obligatory scene in which the rule costs someone something.
    Example. In “Story of Your Life”, Louise Banks tests hypotheses about the aliens’ writing, and learning it has an effect no one planned: she begins to experience her life all at once, including her daughter’s death.
  • A question of scale. Personal choices connect to larger futures, systems or civilisations, and the connection matters more than the size.
    Example. The Ministry for the Future opens with one aid worker surviving a heatwave that kills everyone around him, and follows his life alongside an agency trying to change the world’s climate policy.

Science fiction can be intimate or vast; advanced technology is not required if the premise is materially reasoned.

Series forms. Space opera commonly runs as a serial; Asimov’s robot stories and much short-form science fiction are episodic within a shared rule set.

Examples.

  • I, Robot (Isaac Asimov, 1950). The stories declare three Laws of Robotics and then keep them absolutely: each story’s problem is a robot behaving strangely, and each solution shows the behaviour followed from the Laws. In “Runaround”, the robot Speedy circles a selenium pool on Mercury because a weakly given order and a strengthened self-preservation drive balance; the humans break the deadlock by invoking the First Law, putting one of themselves in danger.
  • The Left Hand of Darkness (Ursula K. Le Guin, 1969). The novum is social and biological: the people of Gethen are ambisexual, taking a sex only during the monthly period of kemmer. The book follows the ripple effects through politics, war and intimacy, seen by Genly Ai, an envoy whose own assumptions are the thing estranged. The rule’s cost is seen in the relationship between Genly and Estraven, which neither can name in the other’s terms until the crossing of the ice.

Climate fiction

Sub-genre of science fiction. Much climate fiction is published as literary fiction or thriller; it is listed here because its premise, an extrapolated climate, is science fiction’s syntax.

Climate fiction, often called cli-fi, makes climate change or ecological disruption consequential to the story. It can be set in the present, near future or distant future.

Core promise. The kept rule, in the form of physical causality: the climate behaves as the science says, and the plot does not escape it by a convenient reprieve. Literary climate fiction adds the promise of significance.

Semantic elements. The heatwave, the flood, the drought and the fire; the climate refugee and the scientist; the drowned coast, the failed harvest and the migrating species; the conference, the policy and the protest.

Syntactic elements. An environmental change forces immediate choices about a problem decades in the making; the story cuts between ordinary lives and global systems; responsibility, denial and collective action create conflict without a single villain; the ending leaves loss and the possibility of change together.

Conventions.

  • A changed environment. Heat, flooding, drought, fire, storms or shifting seasons affect where people live and what they can do.
    Example. In The Drowned World, a warming sun has melted the ice caps, and London is a tropical lagoon where a few scientists survey the drowned city.
  • Consequences in ordinary life. Climate change shapes work, food, housing, health, relationships and plans.
    Example. In Weather, a librarian who answers listeners’ letters for a climate podcast carries the dread home into her marriage and her care of her brother.
  • Unequal exposure. Wealth, geography and political power influence who can adapt and who bears the costs.
    Example. In The Water Knife, the wealthy of a drought-stricken Phoenix live in sealed towers that recycle their water, while those outside buy water by the cup or queue at relief pumps.
  • Interconnected systems. Environmental change affects infrastructure, migration, economies, governments and ecosystems.
    Example. In New York 2140, the flooding of lower Manhattan is tied to the financial markets, where a trader prices the half-drowned property of the intertidal zone.
  • A conflict across timescales. Characters make immediate choices about a problem that has developed over decades.
    Example. In The Overstory, two activists spend about a year living in an ancient redwood to save it, a human span set against the centuries of the tree’s life.
  • Moral and political tension. Responsibility, denial, adaptation and collective action create conflict without requiring a single villain.
    Example. In Solar, a Nobel-winning physicist pursues a solar-energy project while failing at every personal responsibility, so the climate argument and its advocate’s character pull apart.
  • An uncertain future. Endings often leave room for both loss and change.
    Example. Parable of the Sower ends with a handful of survivors founding the first community of Lauren Olamina’s new faith on land in northern California owned by one of the travellers.

The key craft choice is causality: the climate should shape the novel’s decisions and conflicts, not serve only as scenery.

Examples.

  • Flight Behaviour (Barbara Kingsolver, 2012). Monarch butterflies, diverted from their Mexican wintering grounds, settle on a mountain above a Tennessee farm. The book uses climate change as a consequence in one ordinary life: Dellarobia Turnbow’s marriage, church and finances are reshaped by the scientists the butterflies bring, and the novel shows denial and belief as matters of class and community rather than of argument.
  • The Ministry for the Future (Kim Stanley Robinson, 2020). It opens with a heatwave in India that kills millions, a direct confrontation with unequal exposure. The rest of the book follows, through documents and testimonies, the work of a UN body founded in 2025 to speak for future generations; its measures include a carbon-backed currency. The ending is an uncertain improvement, as the convention predicts, reached by collective action rather than a hero.

Dystopian fiction

Sub-genre of science fiction. Darko Suvin classes utopia and its negative, dystopia, as the socio-political sub-genre of science fiction; Margaret Atwood prefers the term speculative fiction for her own dystopias. The anti-utopia, a variety aimed at utopianism itself, is described after the conventions.

Dystopian fiction imagines a society in which a plausible change has made life worse. Its central question is often: what happens when a familiar value, fear or institution is taken to an extreme?

Core promise. The kept rule, applied to a society: the regime’s logic is consistent, and the protagonist does not escape it by a convenient flaw. The genre often adds significance, since the society is a lens on the reader’s own.

Semantic elements. The state, the party and the corporation; surveillance devices; the uniform, the ration and the identity card; the controlled language; the forbidden book or diary; the underground resistance.

Syntactic elements. An insider who knows the rules begins to question them; a relationship, memory or document breaches the official story; private resistance grows into risk; the system responds; the ending often leaves the society intact even if the protagonist survives.

Conventions.

  • An oppressive social order. A state, corporation, caste system or other institution controls important parts of daily life.
    Example. In The Hunger Games, the Capitol rules its twelve districts through an annual lottery that sends two children from each to fight to the death on television.
  • An ideal’s hidden cost. The society promises safety, equality, prosperity or order while sacrificing freedom, dignity or truth.
    Example. Brave New World delivers stability and happiness through conditioning, castes and a drug, soma, at the cost of everything that could make anyone unhappy, from art to family.
  • Systems of control. Surveillance, propaganda, censorship, restricted movement, engineered scarcity and control of language or reproduction are frequent tools.
    Example. In Severance, a company surgically divides its employees’ memories, so that the self who works never knows the self who goes home.
  • A divided population. Privilege and harm are distributed unequally, even when the society claims to serve everyone.
    Example. In Klara and the Sun, children whose parents have had them genetically “lifted” are educated for the best universities, and a boy who has not been lifted finds those doors closed.
  • An insider protagonist. The main character knows the society’s rules well enough to reveal how they work, then begins to question them.
    Example. In Fahrenheit 451, Guy Montag is a fireman whose job is burning books, and he begins to question it after a woman chooses to burn with her library.
  • A breach in the official story. A discovery, relationship, memory or personal loss exposes the gap between what people are told and what they experience.
    Example. In The Giver, Jonas learns what “release” means when he watches his father kill an unwanted newborn twin, and the community’s gentle language falls apart.
  • Resistance with consequences. Defiance may begin as a private act; escaping or defeating the system is rarely simple.
    Example. In Catching Fire, Katniss’s defiance in the previous Games inspires uprisings in the districts, and the Capitol answers by sending her back into the arena.
  • An uneasy ending. Even when the protagonist survives or wins something, the broader society may remain intact.
    Example. Never Let Me Go ends with Kathy about to begin her own donations, and nothing suggests that the system will change.

The anti-utopia is a variety of dystopia aimed at utopianism itself rather than at a present-day tendency. Lyman Tower Sargent defines it as a society the author intends as “a criticism of utopianism or of some particular eutopia”, a eutopia being his word for a society meant to be seen as better than the reader’s own (“The Three Faces of Utopianism Revisited”, 1994). Its regime is sincere and usually succeeds: the society delivers the happiness, equality or stability it promised, and the horror lies in the price of that success. The regime’s spokesperson makes a correspondingly strong case, the citizens are often content, and the protagonist resists by reclaiming what the plan excluded, as John the Savage in Brave New World claims “the right to be unhappy”. The anti-utopia rarely proposes a better order, which separates it from the critical dystopia that keeps hope open (Tom Moylan, Scraps of the Untainted Sky, 2000). In Russian and much Central European criticism, anti-utopia is the usual name for the whole sub-genre.

Examples.

  • Nineteen Eighty-Four (George Orwell, 1949). Winston Smith works at the Ministry of Truth rewriting the past, the insider position the convention requires. His diary and his affair with Julia are the breach; O’Brien’s trap and Room 101 are the system’s response. The book keeps its rule to the end, since Winston is not martyred but converted to loving Big Brother, though its appendix on Newspeak, written in the past tense, hints that the regime did not last.
  • The Handmaid’s Tale (Margaret Atwood, 1985). Gilead controls reproduction, and Offred narrates from inside it. Atwood said she included nothing that had not already happened somewhere, which grounds the novum in record. The ending is doubly uneasy: Offred steps into a van that may mean rescue or arrest, and the “Historical Notes”, an academic conference in 2195, show that Gilead fell and that the scholars who study it still treat her testimony with condescension.

Post-apocalyptic fiction

Sub-genre of science fiction.

Post-apocalyptic fiction explores life after a catastrophe has severely disrupted a society’s institutions and resources.

Core promise. The kept rule of scarcity, often combined with the threat met: survival is confronted directly, and the world does not restore itself by convenience.

Semantic elements. The ruined city and the abandoned road; the cache of supplies; the cart, the gun and the last tin of food; the scavenger band and the settlement; the relics of the old world.

Syntactic elements. Survivors move through a broken world in search of safety; each encounter tests trust; groups improvise new rules and hierarchies; the plot asks what should be preserved, remade or abandoned.

Conventions.

  • A broken ordinary world. Infrastructure, law, supply chains or shared knowledge no longer work as expected.
    Example. In The Day of the Triffids, one night’s meteor shower blinds most of the population, and Bill Masen, who missed it because his eyes were bandaged, wakes to a silent hospital in a city of the blind.
  • Survival choices. Food, shelter, medicine, movement and trust become immediate concerns.
    Example. In Oryx and Crake, Snowman, perhaps the last natural-born human, rations food and hides from the sun while he watches over a new, engineered species.
  • New social arrangements. Groups invent rules, hierarchies and obligations amid scarcity.
    Example. In Mad Max: Fury Road, Immortan Joe rules the Citadel by controlling its water and keeps women as breeders, and the plot begins when they escape.
  • The past as a presence. Ruins, memories and lost skills measure what has changed.
    Example. In A Canticle for Leibowitz, monks centuries after a nuclear war preserve relics of the old world, among them a shopping list, without knowing what they mean.
  • Rebuilding or refusal. The plot asks what should be preserved, remade or abandoned.
    Example. The Last of Us ends with Joel saving Ellie from the surgery that might have produced a vaccine, choosing one person over the chance of rebuilding.

The cause may be climatic, biological, technological or unexplained; a post-apocalyptic world is not automatically a dystopia.

Examples.

  • The Road (Cormac McCarthy, 2006). The catastrophe is never explained, which shifts attention entirely to survival choices: a father and son push a cart south through ash, hoarding food and two bullets. The new social arrangements are cannibal gangs, against which the father defines himself and his son as the good guys who carry the fire. The father dies, and the ending hands the boy to a family that may be good, keeping the question of what survives open.
  • Station Eleven (Emily St. John Mandel, 2014). A flu pandemic ends the old world in weeks, and twenty years on a travelling company performs Shakespeare to scattered settlements. Its caravan’s motto, borrowed from Star Trek: Voyager, holds that survival alone is not enough. The book answers the genre’s question by preserving art, and cuts between the collapse and the years after it to make the past a presence.

Utopian fiction

Sub-genre of science fiction. The form is older than science fiction, beginning with Thomas More’s Utopia (1516); Suvin’s classification places it within science fiction as its socio-political sub-genre.

Utopian fiction imagines and tests a social arrangement intended to be better than familiar alternatives.

Core promise. The kept rule, applied to a society’s principles: the arrangement must work, or fail, by its own logic. Critical utopias, a term Tom Moylan introduced in Demand the Impossible (1986), add the promise of significance by leaving improvement contested.

Semantic elements. The island, the valley or the planet set apart; the visitor and the guide; the institutions of property, labour, education and family; the founding document.

Syntactic elements. A visitor or newcomer is shown how the society works; questions and comparisons expose its governing principle; a conflict tests whether the ideal can adapt to dissent or change.

Conventions.

  • An organised alternative. Institutions, customs and material conditions express a social ideal.
    Example. In News from Nowhere, a future England without money, prisons or central government has turned the Houses of Parliament into a store for dung.
  • A visible governing principle. Readers can see what the society values and how it puts those values into practice.
    Example. In Herland, an all-female society that has reproduced without men for two thousand years organises everything, from education to forestry, around motherhood.
  • An explanatory encounter. A visitor, newcomer or conflict often reveals how the system works.
    Example. In Looking Backward, Julian West sleeps from 1887 to 2000, and his host, Dr Leete, explains to him how Boston’s new economy works.
  • Tradeoffs and exclusions. The story asks whose interests the arrangement serves and what it may cost.
    Example. In “The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas”, the city’s happiness depends on one child kept in misery in a cellar, and the citizens who cannot accept the bargain leave.
  • A test of viability. Conflict exposes whether the ideal can adapt to disagreement or change.
    Example. Island ends with the utopian society of Pala overrun by a neighbouring state that wants its oil.

Some utopias present a settled model; critical utopias leave improvement unfinished and contested. Utopian fiction has two negative counterparts: dystopian fiction, which criticises the reader’s present through a worse society, and the anti-utopia, described in that entry, which criticises the utopian project itself.

Examples.

  • Utopia (Thomas More, 1516). The explanatory encounter is the whole form: the traveller Raphael Hythloday describes to More’s own persona the island’s common property, six-hour working day and religious tolerance. The name is a pun on Greek “no place” and “good place”, and the criticism of England’s enclosures in Book I makes the island an argument about the reader’s own society.
  • The Dispossessed (Ursula K. Le Guin, 1974). Subtitled An Ambiguous Utopia, it alternates between the anarchist moon Anarres and the propertied planet Urras through the physicist Shevek. The book tests its ideal by showing Anarres developing unofficial hierarchies and conformity that block Shevek’s work, so the utopia’s principle is kept and its practice questioned, which is the critical utopia’s design.

Horror

Horror seeks fear, dread, disgust or profound unease by confronting characters with threats to body, identity or reality.

Core promise: the threat met. Series and Genre (Chapter 17) groups horror with the thriller and the adventure. The fit is looser than for the thriller: horror’s readers accept a threat that is never explained and often one that is never defeated, though they still expect it to be faced. The obligatory scene is the victim, or the hero, at the mercy of the threat.

Semantic elements. The haunted house, the isolated town and the cellar; the monster, the ghost, the possessed child and the killer; the warning nobody heeds; darkness, blood and the body changed.

Syntactic elements. Normality is established; a threat intrudes; signs accumulate while characters disbelieve; isolation narrows the means of escape; revelation is controlled so that the unknown stays frightening; a confrontation ends with survival, sacrifice or the threat’s return.

Conventions.

  • A destabilising threat. A being, condition, place or possibility violates a sense of safety.
    Example. In It, the threat takes the shape of whatever each child in a small Maine town fears most, and returns every twenty-seven years.
  • Controlled revelation. Characters and readers learn enough to fear what remains unknown.
    Example. In Psycho, the killer’s mother is heard and glimpsed but not seen until the final discovery in the cellar.
  • Vulnerability. Isolation, disbelief, limited resources or compromised trust narrows the options for escape.
    Example. In Misery, a novelist injured in a car crash is nursed by his most devoted fan in her isolated farmhouse, and he cannot walk away from her.
  • Atmosphere and sensory detail. Sound, space, bodily sensation and anticipation carry tension.
    Example. The Haunting of Hill House makes the house frightening through its sounds and spaces, such as the cold spot outside the nursery and the pounding on the bedroom doors at night.
  • A boundary crossed. The threat often blurs human and monstrous, living and dead, or natural and unnatural.
    Example. Frankenstein’s creature is made from the dead and brought to life, and the book’s horror lies as much in his humanity as in his monstrousness.
  • A confrontation with cost. Survival may require sacrifice, and explanation may not restore security.
    Example. In The Exorcist, Father Karras frees the girl by challenging the demon to take him instead, then throwing himself from her window.

The source of horror may be supernatural, psychological, social or entirely material.

Examples.

  • Dracula (Bram Stoker, 1897). Controlled revelation is built into the form: the story is told in diaries, letters and newspaper cuttings, so each character knows only part of the threat and the reader assembles it. The band of hunters meets the threat directly in the final chase back to Transylvania, where Harker and Quincey Morris destroy the Count at sunset, and Morris’s death is the cost.
  • The Shining (Stephen King, 1977). Isolation is total: the Torrance family are snowed in as winter caretakers of the Overlook Hotel. The hotel’s threat works through Jack’s existing alcoholism and anger, so the boundary between possession and psychology blurs. In the novel, unlike Kubrick’s film, the confrontation ends with the neglected boiler exploding and destroying the hotel, with Jack’s last flicker of self-recognition as the cost.

Gothic fiction

Sub-genre of horror. Historically the Gothic came first: it began with Horace Walpole’s The Castle of Otranto (1764) and is an ancestor of modern horror. It is also a mode that shapes romance, historical and literary fiction.

Gothic fiction turns place, inheritance, secrecy and threatened boundaries into sources of dread.

Core promise. The Gothic novel proper often makes the promise of the threat met; the Gothic romance adds the promise of union. Used as a mode, it carries its conventions without either promise.

Semantic elements. The castle, abbey, mansion or decaying house; the portrait, the locked room and the hidden passage; the usurper, the tyrant and the persecuted heroine; the family curse; the storm.

Syntactic elements. A vulnerable newcomer enters a charged place; the past presses on the present through an inheritance, a crime or a dead predecessor; signs suggest the supernatural, which may be confirmed or explained away; the house’s secret is exposed and the house is often destroyed.

Conventions.

  • A charged setting. A house, landscape or institution seems to preserve past violence or concealment.
    Example. In “The Fall of the House of Usher”, a fissure runs down the front of the family mansion, and at the end the house splits and sinks into the tarn with the last of the family.
  • An encroaching past. Family history, guilt or a suppressed event presses on the present.
    Example. In Jane Eyre, Rochester’s past is literally upstairs: the wife he married in Jamaica is confined in Thornfield’s attic.
  • Uncertain threat. The supernatural may be real, imagined or deliberately left ambiguous.
    Example. The Turn of the Screw never settles whether the ghosts at Bly are real or the governess’s imagination.
  • Vulnerability and confinement. Characters face isolation, coercion or limited means of escape.
    Example. In The Mysteries of Udolpho, Emily St. Aubert is held in a remote castle by her aunt’s husband, who wants her property, and its terrors, including what lies behind a black veil, are finally explained away.
  • Boundary crossing. The familiar becomes strange; life and death, safety and danger, or self and other blur.
    Example. In Strange Case of Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde, a respected doctor’s other self takes over his body until he can no longer return.

Examples.

  • The Castle of Otranto (Horace Walpole, 1764). It established the genre’s semantics in one stroke: a usurping prince named Manfred, a castle with secret passages, a persecuted young woman fleeing through vaults, and a supernatural past that will not stay buried, beginning when a giant helmet falls from the sky and crushes Manfred’s heir. Walpole first presented it as the translation of an old Italian text, printed at Naples in 1529 and found in the library of an ancient Catholic family; the Gothic kept the found document as a device.
  • Rebecca (Daphne du Maurier, 1938). The unnamed second Mrs de Winter arrives at Manderley, where the dead first wife’s presence is preserved by the housekeeper Mrs Danvers. The book varies the Gothic’s supernatural doubt into psychological dread, then reveals a material secret, that Maxim killed Rebecca. The house burns at the end, the Gothic’s usual destruction of its charged setting.

Adventure fiction

Adventure fiction follows characters pursuing a goal through danger, unfamiliar places or a series of demanding challenges. Its momentum comes from what they do, discover and risk.

Core promise: the threat met. The danger the journey sets up will be confronted directly. The obligatory scene is the hero at the mercy of the threat, which in adventure is often the environment rather than a villain.

Semantic elements. The map, the ship and the expedition; the island, the jungle, the desert and the sea; the treasure, the lost city and the way home; the guide, the rival and the betrayer; the storm, the shipwreck and the trap.

Syntactic elements. A concrete objective sends the protagonist beyond familiar knowledge and resources; obstacles escalate; each success reveals a harder problem; allies and rivals shift; the climax tests the skills and relationships the journey built.

Conventions.

  • A concrete objective. The protagonist may seek a person, place, object, escape route or way home.
    Example. In King Solomon’s Mines, Sir Henry Curtis sets out to find his lost brother, and an old map drawn in blood shows the way to the mines.
  • A journey into the unknown. Travel or exploration takes characters beyond their usual knowledge and resources.
    Example. In Heart of Darkness, Marlow’s steamer journey up an African river takes him to a station where the rules of his company and his culture no longer seem to hold.
  • External obstacles. Terrain, weather, enemies, limited supplies and time pressure force action.
    Example. In The Old Man and the Sea, Santiago lands the great marlin after three days, then loses it to sharks on the way home.
  • Escalating stakes. Each success reveals a harder problem or raises the cost of failure.
    Example. In Kidnapped, David Balfour is kidnapped onto a ship, shipwrecked and made a witness to a murder, and he crosses the Highlands as a suspect, each danger worse than the last.
  • Resourceful problem solving. Characters survive through skill, improvisation, courage and help from others.
    Example. On his island in Robinson Crusoe, Crusoe salvages what he can from the wreck, then teaches himself to make pots, grow grain and bake bread.
  • Allies and rivals. Companions, guides, competitors and antagonists complicate the journey.
    Example. In The Lost World, the rival professors Challenger and Summerlee quarrel throughout the expedition, until the plateau’s dinosaurs prove Challenger right.
  • Discovery and change. What the characters learn often changes what they want or who they become.
    Example. In The Call of the Wild, a dog stolen from a California home and sold for the Klondike learns the harsh rules of the trail and ends by running with a wolf pack.
  • A decisive confrontation. The climax tests the abilities and relationships developed along the way.
    Example. Moby-Dick ends in a three-day chase in which the whale kills Ahab and sinks his ship, leaving only Ishmael alive.

An adventure works best when its obstacles demand meaningful choices, so the journey changes the characters as well as their circumstances.

Examples.

  • Treasure Island (Robert Louis Stevenson, 1883). The map from Billy Bones’s sea chest supplies the concrete objective, and the voyage of the Hispaniola the journey. Long John Silver is the convention of shifting allies and rivals in one person: cook, mutineer leader, hostage-taker and finally protector of Jim. The book varies the objective at the end: Ben Gunn has already dug up the treasure, so the climax at the empty pit turns on alliances rather than discovery.
  • The Martian (Andy Weir, 2011). Stranded on Mars, Mark Watney faces the environment as the threat, and the book makes the escalating stakes visible in its log entries, each of which solves one survival problem (water, food, communication, distance). The decisive confrontation is the rescue, improvised in orbit: the crew blow the Hermes’s airlock to close the gap, and Beck, on a tether, catches Watney. Watney’s own plan to fly the last stretch by puncturing his glove is vetoed in the novel and kept only in the film.

Five further adventure novels show how far the genre reaches into others: the race against time, the quest fantasy, the survival story for young readers, the techno-thriller and the literary fable. The table gives what drives the adventure in each.

Novel Author What drives the adventure
Around the World in Eighty Days Jules Verne A wager turns travel itself into a race against time.
The Hobbit J. R. R. Tolkien A reluctant traveller joins a quest and grows more resourceful with each obstacle.
Hatchet Gary Paulsen A thirteen-year-old boy, stranded after a plane crash, must learn to survive alone in the wilderness.
Jurassic Park Michael Crichton Visitors must escape an island after its safety systems fail.
Life of Pi Yann Martel A shipwreck leaves a sixteen-year-old boy adrift in a lifeboat with a Bengal tiger.

In each the danger the journey sets up is met directly, which is the adventure’s promise, while the neighbouring genre supplies the setting and the stakes.

Western fiction

Western fiction uses frontier settings and conflicts over land, law, settlement and violence, most often in the American West of the later nineteenth century.

Core promise. None of its own, since the Western is defined by setting. The traditional Western makes the promise of the threat met, with the showdown as its obligatory scene; the revisionist Western often declines it and makes the promise of significance instead.

Semantic elements. The open range, the desert and the frontier town; the horse, the gun, the cattle drive and the railroad; the gunfighter, the sheriff, the rancher, the homesteader and Indigenous peoples; the saloon and the jail.

Syntactic elements. Among the syntactic accounts Rick Altman cites is Jim Kitses’s in Horizons West (1969), which reads the Western through an opposition between wilderness and civilisation. A stranger arrives where law is weak or contested; competing claims to land escalate; the stranger chooses between protection and coercion; a showdown settles the claim, and the stranger often leaves the community he has made safe.

Conventions.

  • A contested place. Distance, terrain and weak or disputed institutions shape conflict.
    Example. In Blood Meridian, a band of scalp-hunters paid by a Mexican state for Apache scalps soon kills anyone whose scalp will pass, in borderlands no government controls.
  • Competing claims. Settlers, Indigenous peoples, ranchers, workers and authorities have unequal power and incompatible interests.
    Example. In Little Big Man, Jack Crabb is raised by the Cheyenne and moves back and forth between their world and the settlers’, so the reader sees each side’s claims from inside.
  • A test of law and force. Characters face choices about protection, coercion and justice.
    Example. In The Ox-Bow Incident, a posse that will not wait for the sheriff hangs three men for a murder, and learns afterwards that the supposed victim is alive.
  • Movement and encounter. Journeys, pursuit or arrival bring different social orders into contact.
    Example. In True Grit, fourteen-year-old Mattie Ross rides into Indian Territory with a federal marshal to bring her father’s killer to justice.
  • A frontier myth examined. The story may reinforce or challenge ideals of independence and conquest.
    Example. The Virginian did much to make the myth: its cowboy hero is a natural gentleman who wins the showdown with the villain and marries the schoolteacher from the East.

Contemporary Westerns often scrutinise the genre’s older treatment of Indigenous people, race, gender and land.

Examples.

  • Shane (Jack Schaefer, 1949). The classic syntax in its plainest form: a stranger rides into a Wyoming valley, sides with the homesteading Starrett family against the cattleman Fletcher, faces Fletcher’s hired gunman in the showdown and rides away wounded. Narration by the boy Bob Starrett, remembering as an adult, turns the gunfighter into a figure of the frontier myth.
  • Lonesome Dove (Larry McMurtry, 1985). Two retired Texas Rangers, Gus McCrae and Woodrow Call, drive cattle from Texas to Montana. McMurtry said he meant to deflate the frontier myth: the drive kills its men in a snake-filled river crossing and in raids, Gus dies of an infected arrow wound, and Call carries his body back to Texas to keep a promise that serves no one. Many readers took the book as a celebration of the myth anyway, which shows how strongly the genre’s semantics carry its old promise.

Modes

Allegory, comedy and satire are modes in Fowler’s sense, carrying conventions into books of any kind without a core promise of their own.

Allegory

A mode. Allegory runs through fable, satire, religious fiction and fantasy.

Allegory tells a literal story whose characters, events and places also form a sustained second level of meaning.

Core promise. None of its own, as a mode. Its readers do expect the correspondence to be sustained, so that an allegory dropped halfway reads as a failure.

Semantic elements. The journey, the city, the beast and the dream frame; characters named for qualities (Christian, Despair); emblematic places such as a slough or a fair.

Syntactic elements. Each event at the literal level answers to one at the second level; the sequence of the story follows the sequence of the argument; the ending completes or complicates both at once.

Conventions.

  • A readable surface story. The action makes sense before its symbolic pattern is interpreted.
    Example. The Buried Giant can be read first as an elderly couple’s journey to find their son through a land where a mist makes everyone forget, before the mist is read as a nation’s chosen forgetting of its wars.
  • Organised correspondences. Details repeatedly point towards moral, political, religious or philosophical ideas.
    Example. In The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe, Aslan gives his life on the Stone Table in place of the traitor Edmund and returns to life, so the story follows the Christian account of atonement, though Lewis himself called the book a “supposal” rather than an allegory.
  • Representative figures. Characters or places may embody a role, value, institution or conflict.
    Example. In Waiting for the Barbarians, the Magistrate, Colonel Joll and the Empire itself are never placed in a real country, so they can stand for any empire and its servants.
  • Interpretive cues. Names, repeated images and parallel events invite readers to connect the two levels.
    Example. In The Scarlet Letter, the A that Hester wears recurs in a meteor that seems to draw the letter across the sky, inviting the reader to read every sign twice.
  • A consequential ending. The resolution completes or complicates the argument carried by the allegory.
    Example. Lord of the Flies ends with a naval officer rescuing the boys from their island war, an adult engaged in a larger war of his own.

The pattern needs enough consistency to reward interpretation without reducing every character to a single fixed meaning.

Examples.

  • The Pilgrim’s Progress (John Bunyan, 1678). Framed as a dream, it follows Christian from the City of Destruction to the Celestial City. The interpretive cues are explicit in the names (Slough of Despond, Vanity Fair, Giant Despair), so that each episode of the journey is a stage of the soul’s salvation, and crossing the river into the city completes both levels.
  • Animal Farm (George Orwell, 1945). The surface story of a farm revolt works as a fable, and the correspondences map it onto the Russian Revolution: Napoleon to Stalin, Snowball to Trotsky, the rewriting of the Seven Commandments to the rewriting of history. The ending closes both levels: the animals look from pigs to men and can no longer tell them apart.

Comedy

A mode in prose fiction, and a full kind in drama, where comedy traditionally ends in marriage.

Comedy exposes incongruity and creates pleasure through humour, social friction or the reversal of expectations. It can accompany almost any genre.

Core promise. None of its own in fiction. Dramatic comedy as a kind traditionally promises a reordering, most often marriages, and a novel in the comic mode often borrows that ending.

Semantic elements. The impostor, the disguise and the misunderstanding; the pompous authority and the servant cleverer than the master; the party, the wedding and the country house; the letter that goes to the wrong person.

Syntactic elements. A character’s self-image or plan clashes with reality; concealment makes the original problem worse; complications escalate through timing; recognition and reversal release the tension into a new arrangement, which may exclude a figure the comedy has punished.

Conventions.

  • A comic mismatch. A character’s self-image, desire or plan clashes with reality.
    Example. In A Confederacy of Dunces, Ignatius J. Reilly, who sees himself as a medieval scholar exiled in a vulgar age, is made by his mother to find a job in New Orleans.
  • Escalating complications. Mistakes, concealment, rivalry or bad timing make the original problem harder to manage.
    Example. In Right Ho, Jeeves, Bertie Wooster’s plans to help his friends end with the shy Gussie Fink-Nottle, nerved with drink, presenting the prizes at a grammar school.
  • Timing and reversals. Dialogue and events change meaning through surprise, repetition or delayed recognition.
    Example. The Importance of Being Earnest saves its reversal for the last moments: the handbag in which Jack was found as a baby makes him Algernon’s elder brother, and the Army Lists show that his name was Ernest all along.
  • Social observation. Manners, status, institutions and group norms become sources of conflict.
    Example. Seinfeld builds episodes from small breaches of manners, such as double-dipping a crisp or passing on an unwanted present.
  • A release or reordering. Many comedies end in recognition, reconciliation or a newly workable arrangement; dark comedy may withhold these.
    Example. In Cold Comfort Farm, Flora Poste sets each member of a gloomy farming family on a happier course, and the novel ends with her own departure by aeroplane with the man she will marry.

The humour can be affectionate, caustic, absurd or bleak. A comic novel still needs stakes that matter to its characters.

Examples.

  • Twelfth Night (William Shakespeare, about 1601). Viola, shipwrecked and disguised as the page Cesario, loves Orsino, who loves Olivia, who loves Cesario: concealment makes the original problem worse. The arrival of Viola’s twin Sebastian turns the confusion into recognition and multiple marriages, the kind’s ending; the steward Malvolio, humiliated by a forged letter, leaves vowing revenge, the figure the reordering excludes.
  • Lucky Jim (Kingsley Amis, 1954). Jim Dixon, a junior lecturer who despises his subject, must please his absurd professor, Welch, to keep his job. Complications escalate to Jim’s public talk on “Merrie England”, which he delivers drunk and in mimicry of his superiors. The comic release is total: he loses the job, gains a better one and leaves with the girl, laughing at the Welches.

Satire

A mode, usually comic. Satire was also a full kind in classical verse, in Horace and Juvenal.

Satire uses humour, irony or absurdity to expose a flaw in people, institutions or society. It can be a novel, play, film or part of another genre.

Core promise. None of its own, as a mode. Its readers expect a legible target: they should be able to tell what is being criticised and why.

Semantic elements. The innocent traveller or newcomer; the absurd institution, rule or ceremony; the caricatured official; the parodied document or speech; the imaginary country.

Syntactic elements. A real tendency is pushed until its logic and consequences show; characters treat the absurd as normal; an outsider’s naïve view exposes the insiders’ assumptions; the plot lets a foolish or harmful idea play out.

Conventions.

  • A clear target. The work criticises a behaviour, belief, institution or use of power.
    Example. In Bleak House, the target is the Court of Chancery: the case of Jarndyce and Jarndyce runs for generations and ends when legal costs have consumed the estate.
  • Exaggeration. A real tendency is pushed far enough to make its logic and consequences visible.
    Example. In Erewhon, illness is punished as a crime and crime is treated as an illness, so the reader sees how much moral judgement Victorian England brought to misfortune.
  • Irony. There is a gap between what characters claim, what they intend and what their actions reveal.
    Example. In Parasite, the rich Park family are polite and generous, and their remarks about the smell of the poor family they employ show what the politeness covers.
  • Incongruity. Things that should clash are treated as normal, exposing the assumptions behind them.
    Example. In Scoop, a newspaper’s nature columnist, mistaken for a novelist with the same surname, is sent to report a war that barely exists, and the press reports it all the same.
  • Caricature or representative characters. A character may embody a social role or attitude and still have depth.
    Example. In Succession, the Roy children are caricatures of inherited wealth, and the series still shows the damage their father has done to each of them.
  • Imitation and parody. Familiar language, genres or public rituals are copied to reveal their weaknesses.
    Example. In Northanger Abbey, Catherine Morland searches a mysterious cabinet at night as a Gothic heroine would, and finds a laundry list.
  • Consequences that expose the flaw. The plot allows a foolish or harmful idea to play out.
    Example. In Dr Strangelove, the Soviet doomsday machine, built to deter an attack, fails to deter because its existence has not yet been announced, and the world ends.

Satire need not be lighthearted; some satire is bleak or unsettling.

Examples.

  • Gulliver’s Travels (Jonathan Swift, 1726). Gulliver’s naïve reports carry the irony. In Lilliput, a war over which end to break an egg exaggerates religious schism into absurdity; among the Houyhnhnms (rational horses), the human-like Yahoos expose humanity at its worst. The satire turns on its narrator at the end, when Gulliver returns home preferring the company of his horses to his family.
  • Catch-22 (Joseph Heller, 1961). The target is military bureaucracy, and the book’s rule states it: a man who asks to be grounded for insanity proves himself sane by asking, so he must fly. Milo Minderbinder’s syndicate, which contracts with the Germans to bomb his own squadron, lets profit-seeking play out to its logical end. Yossarian’s final flight towards neutral Sweden is the one exit the system’s logic leaves.
